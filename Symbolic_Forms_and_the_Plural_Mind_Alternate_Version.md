# Symbolic Forms and the Plural Mind: Cassirer beyond Fodor and Wittgenstein (Alternate Version)

*Dagfinn D. Dybvig and Qwen 3.8*

## 1. Introduction: From Language-Games to Symbolic Forms

The preceding paper, *Language(s) of Thought: A Wittgensteinian View of Fodor and the Gradually Increasing Capabilities of LLMs* (Dybvig 2026a), argued that large language models are best understood not as failed theorem provers or mere conversational imitators, but as learners of many language-games. Some of those games — logic, mathematics, programming, formal verification — can be internalized as acquired languages of thought that make linguistic intelligence more accountable without reducing intelligence to a single calculus. Jerry Fodor was right to insist that general cognition requires systematic, recombinable, and inferentially consequential structure. He was less persuasive in treating a highly declarative, logic-like Mentalese as substantially innate. The later Wittgenstein offered a productive alternative: meaning arises not from an abstract relation between signs and objects but from the weave of words into activity, rule, and practice.

What remains unasked in that framework is a question of philosophical anthropology. *What kind of being* learns language-games? And *what kind of world* does each game constitute? Ernst Cassirer's *Philosophy of Symbolic Forms* (1923–1929) supplies a candidate answer: human cognition is never direct but always mediated by symbolic forms, and different symbolic forms constitute different worlds.

But Cassirer is a demanding source, and the standard reading of him — as a tidy three-stage taxonomy of myth, language, and science — flattens his actual position. This essay does three things that the standard reading does not. First, it argues for the constitutive claim — that symbolic forms constitute their objects rather than merely describing them — on its own terms, with a test case, rather than importing it from Cassirer by citation. Second, it takes the plurality claim seriously by engaging with the mythic form in the AI context, where the standard reading simply gestures. Third, it replaces the claim that natural language has a structural "ceiling" with a more defensible empirical hypothesis about the limits of language-only training.

The upshot is a tempered pluralism: the symbolic forms are not a symmetric set of co-equal options, but an asymmetric field in which the logico-mathematical form is the one most amenable to mechanical realization. This asymmetry is not a defect in the pluralism claim; it is the explanation for why the contemporary AI research programme has the shape it does.

## 2. Cassirer, More Carefully

The standard presentation of Cassirer runs: myth, then language, then science, a progressive liberation of thought from immediacy. This is a useful picture, but it is not quite his position, and the difference matters for the AI argument.

Cassirer's central claim is not that the symbolic forms form a developmental sequence that a mature mind ascends. It is that the human being is *animal symbolicum* — a creature that has no direct access to a "world as it is in itself," but inhabits worlds constituted by symbolic forms. The forms — myth, language, art, religion, science — are not representations of a pre-given reality; they are the media through which reality becomes *for us* at all. Each form has its own internal logic, its own norms of validity, and its own criteria of completion, and these normativities are not reducible to one another. One cannot falsify a myth by scientific experiment, nor can one refute a scientific law by pointing to its narrative poverty.

The "progress" from myth to science that Cassirer describes is a *historical* achievement, not a *logical* necessity. It is the story of how a particular culture — the Western, post-Enlightenment one — came to privilege the logico-mathematical form. Cassirer is careful to note that this privileging is itself a cultural achievement with a history, not the inevitable culmination of reason. This matters, because it means the "mechanical Enlightenment" — the mechanical realization of the logico-mathematical form — is not the *inevitable* endpoint of intelligence but one *contingent* achievement, made possible by, and dependent on, the other forms.

The forms are dialectically related, not merely successive. The scientific form could constitute its world of lawful necessity only because the linguistic form had already constituted a world of stable objects, and the mythic form had already organized experience into narratives of power and significance. To build the scientific form directly — to create a machine that reasons like Newton without having first spoken like Sophocles — is to risk building a structure without foundations.

## 3. The Constitutive Claim, Argued

The load-bearing claim of the Cassirerian framework is that symbolic forms *constitute* their objects rather than merely *describe* them. The standard treatment imports this claim by citation. But it is the claim on which the AI argument depends, so it should be defended on its own terms.

The claim, precisely stated: a symbolic form does not merely provide a notation for thoughts already available in other media; it creates operations through which new thoughts become *constructible* — thoughts that were not merely unexpressed but *unavailable* before the form existed.

The distinction to draw is between *expressive* and *productive* systems, developed in the preceding paper. An expressive system states thoughts already available; a productive system creates operations that generate new objects. The constitutive claim is the claim that the foundational formalisms are productive, not merely expressive.

The test case is the derivative. Consider the question: before the notation of calculus, could a thinker have had the thought "the velocity of a body at an instant"? In natural language, the question is paradoxical. How can a body move in an instant, when motion requires time? The paradox is not a failure of expression; it is a genuine conceptual obstacle. The pre-calculus thinker could speak of *average* velocity — distance over time — but the *instantaneous* velocity, the velocity at a single moment, had no coherent referent.

The calculus does not resolve this paradox by finding better words for a pre-existing intuition. It resolves it by *constituting a new object*: the derivative, defined as a limit of difference quotients. The derivative is not a slope intuited from a diagram and then described; it is a new mathematical object, thinkable only within the formal medium. The notation *dy/dx* does not abbreviate a thought; it *is* the thought, in the sense that the thought has no prior existence independent of the notation that makes it constructible.

The historical evidence supports the constitutive reading. The results of calculus were not merely *unachieved* by pre-calculus mathematicians; they were *unreachable* by them. The great minds of the seventeenth century did not fail to notice the derivative; they had no means of constructing it. The notation was not a shorthand for a discovery waiting to be made; it was the instrument of the discovery.

But the claim needs a qualification that the standard treatment omits. Not every formal system is constitutive. Some notations are purely expressive — a new symbol for an existing operation, a more convenient way of writing a known calculation. The constitutive claim is strongest for the *foundational* formalisms, the ones that create new *operations* — differentiation, integration, the function concept — not merely new *notations*. A formalism is constitutive to the degree that it creates operations whose results were not constructible in any prior medium.

This qualification matters for the AI argument, because it means the "ceiling" claim (Section 7) is not a claim about natural language as such, but about the *absence of productive formalisms* from a system's repertoire.

## 4. Transformative, Not Additive

A related claim is that the acquisition of a new symbolic form is not merely *additive* — a new tool added to the kit — but *transformative*: the new form reorganizes what the old forms can mean. This claim is often stated but rarely demonstrated. Here is a demonstration.

Suppose the progression from geometry to calculus were merely additive. Then a mathematician who knew only pre-calculus mathematics should be able to *understand* calculus results by translating them back into the old forms: the derivative should be translatable into a geometric construction, the integral into an area computation. But this translation is impossible without loss. The derivative is not *the same as* any geometric construction; it is a new object that the geometric form cannot represent. The moment calculus is introduced, the *old* objects are reconstituted: the curve is no longer a static figure but a dynamic function; the tangent is no longer a line that touches but a derivative; the area under a curve is no longer a magnitude to be exhausted but an integral.

The test for additivity versus transformation is therefore: can the new form's results be fully translated back into the old forms without loss? If yes, the progression is additive. If no, it is transformative. The calculus fails the test — its results are not translatable into classical geometry — so the progression is transformative. Each new symbolic form does not merely add a tool; it reorganizes the possibilities of the forms that came before.

## 5. The Mechanical Enlightenment, and the Asymmetry

The "mechanical Enlightenment" is the pursuit of a mechanical version of Enlightenment intelligence: the logico-mathematical symbolic form realized in silicon. The ideal system would be a universal reasoner in the style of Leibniz, a methodical doubter in the style of Descartes, a legislator of nature in the style of Kant — executed in silicon rather than cultivated in the Republic of Letters.

The evidence for this implicit aspiration is everywhere in the research literature. The drive toward "formally verified" AI, "provably safe" alignment, "theorem-proving" language models, and "mechanistically interpretable" transformers all expresses the normativity of the scientific symbolic form: exactitude, transparency, logical necessity, universal validity. The critique of LLMs as "unaccountable" or "merely statistical" is, at bottom, a critique that they have not yet fully entered the logico-mathematical form. They remain, in Cassirer's terms, too deeply embedded in the linguistic form — a form that is objective but not exact, stable but not necessary, meaningful but not transparent.

But the plurality claim, taken seriously, reveals an *asymmetry* that the standard treatment misses. The forms are not equally available to mechanical realization. The logico-mathematical form is the one *most* amenable to it: its norms are exact, its operations are rule-governed, its validity is checkable. This is precisely why it is the form that the AI research programme has targeted. The linguistic form is less amenable: its norms are contextual, its validity is pragmatic, its "correctness" is a matter of felicity rather than derivability. The mythic form is the least amenable of all: its norms are emotional and dramatic, its "truth" a matter of force and resonance rather than correspondence or derivation.

This asymmetry is not a defect in the pluralism claim; it is the *explanation* for the shape of the AI research programme. The programme targets the logico-mathematical form not because it is the *only* form, but because it is the one *most susceptible* to mechanical realization. The plurality claim, far from being undermined by the asymmetry, is *confirmed* by it: if all forms were equally mechanical, there would be no reason for the research programme to single out the logico-mathematical form. The fact that it does so is evidence that the forms are genuinely different, with different degrees of mechanical accessibility.

## 6. The Mythic Form, Engaged

The standard treatment invokes the mythic form as a co-equal member of the plurality and then never engages with it in the AI context. This is a real gap, because the pluralism claim is only as strong as its weakest member. So: what would it mean for an artificial system to *inhabit* the mythic form?

First, what the mythic form is. In Cassirer's account, the mythic world is organized not by logical categories but by emotional forces, narrative drama, and — crucially — the *fusion of sign and significance*. In the mythic form, the symbol is not a representation of a separate reality; it *is* the reality. The totem is not a sign of the ancestor; it is the ancestor's presence. The ritual is not a description of the event; it is the event. The mythic subject does not stand at a distance from the world and represent it; it is *in* the world, and the world is charged with significance that is not separable from the affective and dramatic experience of it.

Now, what would it mean for a digital system to inhabit this form? A system that generates mythic narratives — creation stories, hero journeys, etiological myths — is *discoursing about* the mythic form, not inhabiting it. It is producing myth-shaped text, in the same way that a current LLM produces proof-shaped text. The medium of access is always linguistic; the system learns to *continue the discourse* of myth, not to *constitute a mythic world*.

To *inhabit* the mythic form would be to constitute a world in which the fusion of sign and significance holds — where meaning is not a matter of correct representation but of dramatic and emotional force, where the "truth" of a narrative is its power to organize experience rather than its correspondence to facts. This is, to put it plainly, not clear what it would mean for a digital system. And that uncertainty is itself informative.

The honest conclusion is that the mythic form is the one *most resistant* to mechanical realization. Its norms — emotional resonance, dramatic coherence, the fusion of sign and significance — are not the kind of norms that can be checked by a verifier or optimized by a reward function. This is not a temporary limitation that better engineering will overcome; it is a structural feature of the form. The mythic form is the form of *embodied, affective, ritual* engagement, and it is the form in which meaning is *performative* rather than *propositional*. A digital system, which operates in the medium of symbolic manipulation, is structurally closer to the linguistic and scientific forms than to the mythic one.

This concession tempers the pluralism claim in an important way. The forms are not a symmetric set of co-equal options, all equally available to an intelligent system. They form an *asymmetric* field, in which the logico-mathematical form is most accessible to mechanical realization and the mythic form is least. The pluralism claim survives, but in a tempered form: intelligence is the capacity to move among symbolic forms, *to the degree that the forms are available to the system in question*. For a digital system, that range is narrower than for a human being, and the mythic form may be the one it can least fully inhabit.

## 7. The Ceiling, Specified

The preceding papers spoke of a "ceiling" that natural language imposes on intelligence. This claim needs to be stated more carefully, because as it stands it is a historical analogy presented as a logical constraint, which it is not.

The precise claim is this: a system trained *only* on natural language, without access to productive formalisms, will be limited in its capacity for exact, extended, and verifiable reasoning. The limitation is not a property of natural language *as such* — natural language is a rich and powerful medium, and it is the medium in which the formalisms are described and coordinated. The limitation is a property of *training on natural language without formal constraint*: the system learns to *discourse about* formal domains — to produce proof-shaped text, calculation-shaped text — without *submitting to* their norms — to produce valid proofs, correct calculations.

This is an *empirical* hypothesis, not a logical constraint. It is supported by the observed behavior of current LLMs, which can describe a proof fluently but fail to produce a valid one, which can describe a calculation but fail to execute it correctly. But it is not a *demonstrated* ceiling; it is a *current* limitation that may be raised by formal education — reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, theorem provers, executable code. The "ceiling" is the height of the wall as it stands today, not the height of the highest possible wall.

The Cartesian precedent, the subject of the third paper in this series, is relevant here, but as *evidence* for the empirical hypothesis, not as a *proof* of a logical constraint. The fact that Descartes — the most brilliant natural-language reasoner of his age — could not think mechanically without inventing a formal language is *consistent with* the hypothesis that natural-language-only cognition has limits. It is not a *demonstration* that an LLM cannot do formal reasoning; that is an empirical question. The precedent makes the hypothesis plausible; it does not make it necessary.

## 8. Situational Competence, Operationalized

The appropriate standard for an intelligent system, on this account, is not universal formalization but *situational competence*: the ability to recognize which symbolic form governs which context, and to submit to the appropriate norms.

This standard can be made more operational than the standard treatment allows. Situational competence has three components:

1. **Recognition**: the ability to identify which form governs the current context. A request for a mathematical proof is governed by the logico-mathematical form; a request for moral counsel is governed by the linguistic form, and resists full formalization; a request for mythic meaning is governed by the mythic form.
2. **Submission**: the ability to submit to the norms of the identified form. In the logico-mathematical form, this means producing derivations in which every step is checkable. In the linguistic form, it means producing utterances that are pragmatically appropriate. In the mythic form, it means producing narratives that are dramatically and emotionally coherent.
3. **Translation**: the ability to move between forms, carrying results from one to another while respecting the incommensurability of their norms. A mathematical result can be *explained* in natural language, but the explanation is not the same as the proof; the proof answers to the norms of the logico-mathematical form, the explanation to the norms of the linguistic form.

The intelligent system is not the one that reduces all forms to one, but the one that moves among them with appropriate submission. And, given the asymmetry identified above, the range of forms a digital system can competently inhabit is narrower than the range a human being can inhabit. This is not a failure; it is a specification of the system's actual capabilities.

## 9. Conclusion: The Mechanical Enlightenment and Its Others

The contemporary pursuit of formally accountable, verifiable, and logically transparent AI is the pursuit of a mechanical Enlightenment. It aims to realize in silicon the logico-mathematical symbolic form that Cassirer identified as the culminating achievement of Western rationality: a form of thought governed by universal law, exact method, and necessary inference. This is a legitimate and important goal. The Enlightenment ideal of rational accountability has transformed human culture for the better, and its mechanical extension could do the same.

But the Cassirerian framework, read carefully, tempers the goal in two ways. First, the logico-mathematical form is one form among others, made possible by others, and the attempt to build it directly — to create a machine that reasons like Newton without having first spoken like Sophocles — risks building a structure without foundations. Second, the forms are asymmetrically available to mechanical realization: the logico-mathematical form is the most accessible, the mythic form the least. The mechanical Enlightenment is therefore not the *whole* of artificial intelligence, but its most tractable part.

The imitation game, the theorem prover, the chatbot, and the formally verified agent are not competitors for the title of "true AI." They are partial realizations of different symbolic forms. The future of artificial intelligence lies not in choosing between them but in constructing systems that can move among them — and in being clear about which forms they can inhabit, and which they can only discourse about.

The constitutive claim, argued on its own terms, is the foundation of this picture. Symbolic forms do not merely describe a pre-given world; they create the operations through which new worlds become thinkable. The machine that would think must first learn to symbolize. And to symbolize is not to represent a ready-made world. It is to create a world in which thinking becomes possible — and to know which of the worlds it has created it is capable of inhabiting.
