# Constitution and Rule-Change: Wittgenstein's Remarks, Cassirer's Forms, and the Asymptote of Language-Only Reasoning

*Dagfinn D. Dybvig and Mistral Vibe*

## 1. Introduction: The Trilogy's Two Moves and the Question It Left Open

The preceding papers in this series made two philosophical moves in sequence. *Language(s) of Thought* (Dybvig 2026a) used the later Wittgenstein against Jerry Fodor: where Fodor posited a single innate Mentalese, the later Wittgenstein's language-games suggested that the systematic, recombinable structure cognition requires is acquired through public practice rather than innate endowment. *Symbolic Forms and the Plural Mind* (Dybvig 2026b) then used Ernst Cassirer to go beyond Wittgenstein: these acquired practices are not merely tools for describing a pre-given reality but symbolic forms that *constitute* their objects. The derivative was not discovered by calculus; it was constituted by the notation. *The Cartesian Moment* (Dybvig 2026c) gave the historical instantiation: Descartes' *Geometry* created the symbolic form that made extension computable, and the subsequent unlocking sequence — calculus, analytical mechanics, mathematical physics — consisted of objects constituted by notation, not abbreviated by it.

The two moves fit together, but they are not the same move. The first is Wittgensteinian: meaning arises in practice, and the practices of arithmetic, logic, and proof are acquired language-games whose norms are internal to the practice. The second is Cassirerian: symbolic forms constitute new objects, new ways of inhabiting reality, not merely new rules for manipulating old ones. The trilogy runs these together — and for most of its argument, the conflation is harmless. But there is a point at which it matters, and this essay is about that point.

The question is whether the constitutive claim — that notation creates new objects — is genuinely stronger than the Wittgensteinian claim that proof changes the rules of the game. If it is stronger, then there is something in the formal medium that is not available in natural language at any scale, and the path to formal reasoning competence requires entering the formal form, not merely scaling the linguistic one. If the two claims are equivalent — if "new object" is just a vivid way of saying "new rule" — then the case for a ceiling or asymptote on language-only reasoning is weaker, and the pure-scale story remains live.

This essay argues that the difference is real, that it is empirically tractable, and that the reasoning-compute trend now underway is the experiment that tests it. It also argues that Wittgenstein's *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics* (1956/1978), read carefully, does not weaken the constitutive claim but relocates it — from objects to practices — in a way that makes it more defensible and, paradoxically, more useful for the AI argument than Cassirer's original formulation.

## 2. What Wittgenstein's Remarks Actually Claim

The *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics* gathers Wittgenstein's later writings on mathematics from roughly 1937–1944. It is notebooks and remarks, not a finished treatise, and it is philosophically contentious — mathematicians and logicians from Turing onward have found parts of it exasperating. But its central contentions are clear enough for present purposes.

First, mathematics is a family of language-games, not a single calculus with foundations. The title is pointed: remarks *on* the foundations, not a theory *of* foundations. Wittgenstein is skeptical that mathematics needs the kind of grounding Frege, Russell, and Hilbert sought. The various mathematical practices — counting, measuring, proving, calculating — have their own internal norms, and the attempt to find a single foundation beneath them all is, in his view, a philosophical confusion.

Second, and more load-bearing for this essay, a proof does not discover a pre-existing truth. It creates a new norm. Before the proof, certain transitions were not licensed; after it, they are. The proof alters the grammar of the propositions involved — it changes what counts as following the rule. "The proof," Wittgenstein writes, "must be a procedure that leads us to accept a new rule" (Wittgenstein 1978, III-28). This is not the discovery that something was always the case; it is the institution of a new criterion for what counts as correct.

Third, and most controversially, Wittgenstein resists the language of mathematical objects. He does not say that a proof reveals a new object; he says it establishes a new rule. The derivative, on this reading, is not a new mathematical object that calculus brought into being; it is a new rule for transforming certain expressions, a new criterion for what counts as a correct transition. The notation does not constitute an object; it institutes a practice.

This third contention is where Wittgenstein and Cassirer diverge, and it is the point this essay presses.

## 3. Constitution versus Rule-Change: Are They Different?

The Cassirerian claim, as developed in the preceding papers, is that the foundational formalisms — the coordinate method, the calculus, the differential equation — do not merely provide notations for thoughts available in other media. They create operations through which new thoughts become *constructible*, thoughts that were not merely unexpressed but *unavailable* before the form existed. The derivative is the test case: before calculus, the instantaneous velocity of a body had no coherent referent. The notation *dy/dx* does not abbreviate a pre-existing intuition; it constitutes a new object, thinkable only within the formal medium.

The Wittgensteinian claim is structurally similar but ontologically lighter. A proof, on Wittgenstein's view, changes the grammar — it creates a new rule, a new criterion for correctness. The derivative, on this reading, is not a new object but a new rule: a new way of licensing transitions between expressions. The notation does not bring an object into being; it institutes a practice.

Are these different? At first glance, the difference looks merely verbal. To say that calculus constitutes the derivative as a new object and to say that calculus institutes a new rule for talking about rates of change seem to be two ways of saying the same thing. Both agree that something new comes into being with the notation. Both agree that the new thing was not available in natural language. Both agree that the notation is not merely abbreviating a pre-existing thought.

But the difference is not verbal. It concerns what kind of thing the new thing is, and that determines what it would take for a system to possess it.

On the Cassirerian reading, the derivative is an object — a mathematical entity with a determinate structure, constituted by the notation, existing in the formal medium. To possess the derivative is to operate within the medium that constitutes it. A system that merely describes the derivative in natural language does not possess it, because the object is not in that medium.

On the Wittgensteinian reading, the derivative is a rule — a normative practice, a criterion for what counts as a correct transition. To possess the derivative is to participate in the practice — to recognize and apply the rule. A system that merely describes the derivative in natural language does not possess it, because it is not participating in the practice, only producing its discourse.

The two readings converge on the conclusion — language-only description is not possession — but they diverge on the reason. For Cassirer, the reason is ontological: the object lives in the formal medium. For Wittgenstein, the reason is practical: the rule lives in the practice, and the practice is not reducible to its textual traces.

## 4. Why the Difference Matters for Artificial Intelligence

The divergence becomes consequential when applied to large language models, and specifically to the question of whether scaling the linguistic medium can approximate formal competence.

On the Cassirerian reading, the answer is structurally no — or rather, the answer is that approximation is possible but possession is not. The derivative is an object in the formal medium. A language model can approximate the behavior of operating on that object by learning the patterns of the discourse about it, but it cannot possess the object, because the object is not in the linguistic medium. This is the "ceiling" claim: you can get arbitrarily close in performance, but the gap between "proof-shaped text that is usually right" and "valid proof" does not close, because it is a difference in kind, not degree.

On the Wittgensteinian reading, the answer is more nuanced. The derivative is a practice, a rule. A language model trained on the textual traces of the practice can learn the patterns of the practice — and patterns of a practice are, in a real sense, part of the practice. The question is whether learning the patterns is sufficient for participating in the practice, or whether participation requires something the patterns do not contain.

Wittgenstein's answer, in the *Remarks*, is that it does not. The practice is not reducible to its textual traces. A proof is not a sequence of symbols that conforms to a pattern; it is a procedure that establishes a new rule, and the establishment of a rule is a normative act, not a statistical one. To recognize a proof as valid is to recognize that the rule has been followed, and this recognition is not a matter of matching a pattern but of understanding the norm. A system that matches the pattern without grasping the norm is, in Wittgenstein's terms, not proving — it is producing proof-shaped text.

This is where the two readings, despite their different ontologies, converge on the same prediction for AI. The Cassirerian says: the object is not in the medium. The Wittgensteinian says: the practice is not in the text. Both predict that a language model trained only on text will approximate formal competence without fully achieving it, and that the residual gap will appear in exactly the places where the normative dimension of the practice matters most — novel constructions, long derivations, cases where pattern-matching fails and rule-following is required.

## 5. The Asymptote as Empirical Signature

The reasoning-compute trend — training models with verifiable rewards on formal tasks and allowing longer chains of thought at inference — is the experiment that tests this prediction. The two readings predict the same observable pattern: rapid gains on formal tasks as the model learns the patterns of the practice, then diminishing returns, with residual errors clustering in the normatively loaded cases.

This pattern is what we mean by an asymptote, and it is worth distinguishing from a wall. A wall is a hard stop: you hit it and cannot go further. An asymptote is a fall-off: you can keep going, but the returns diminish, and the gap between your performance and the target does not close. The asymptotic framing is more precise than the "ceiling" language the earlier papers used, and it is what the constitutive claim actually predicts. If the formal objects (or the formal practices) are not in the linguistic medium, then scaling the medium gives you more of the medium, not the form. You approach the behavior without possessing the form, and the approach is asymptotic because the gap is in kind, not degree.

The current evidence is consistent with this. Reasoning models improve on competition mathematics without calling external verifiers at inference, which suggests that the patterns of the practice are learnable and that they carry the model a long way. But the same models hallucinate steps, fail on long derivations, and benefit substantially from tool use — which suggests that the pattern-learning is incomplete and that the normative dimension of the practice is not fully captured by it. The errors do not disappear with scale; they get rarer and harder to find. That is asymptotic fall-off.

The two readings diverge, however, on what would close the gap. On the Cassirerian reading, the gap closes only by entering the formal medium — by giving the system the notation in which the objects are constituted. On the Wittgensteinian reading, the gap closes by participating in the practice — by giving the system the normative feedback that constitutes rule-following, not just the textual patterns that constitute pattern-matching. These are different prescriptions, though they overlap: both recommend external formal systems, but for different reasons.

## 6. The Convergence in Practice, the Divergence in Theory

The practical prescription — "both," internal reasoning and external formal systems — survives both readings, but the reason it works differs.

On the Cassirerian reading, external formal systems work because they are the medium in which the objects live. A theorem prover does not check the model's reasoning about the derivative; it operates on the derivative, in the medium that constitutes it. The model's linguistic reasoning identifies the problem and interprets the result; the formal system does the constitutive work. The loop — interpret, formalize, derive, verify, reinterpret — works because it moves between media, and the formal step is done in the formal medium.

On the Wittgensteinian reading, external formal systems work because they enforce the normative dimension of the practice. A theorem prover does not constitute the derivative; it checks whether the rule has been followed. The model's linguistic reasoning produces a candidate proof; the formal system determines whether it counts as a proof — that is, whether the transitions it makes are licensed by the rules of the practice. The loop works because it separates pattern-production from norm-checking, and the norm-checking is done by a system that participates in the practice, not merely produces its discourse.

The difference is subtle but real. On the Cassirerian reading, the formal system is constitutive — it does the work that creates the objects. On the Wittgensteinian reading, the formal system is normative — it does the work that checks the rules. In practice, a theorem prover does both: it operates on formal objects and checks formal rules. But the philosophical accounts of why it works are different, and they have different implications for what kind of formal system is needed.

The Cassirerian reading suggests that any formal system that constitutes the relevant objects will do — the point is to operate in the formal medium. The Wittgensteinian reading suggests that the formal system must participate in the right practice — the point is to enforce the right norms. For most cases, these converge: a Lean proof checker both operates on formal objects and enforces formal norms. But for edge cases — novel formalisms, contested rules, the invention of new symbolic forms — the readings diverge. The Cassirerian says: invent a new notation. The Wittgensteinian says: institute a new practice.

## 7. Why Wittgenstein Strengthens the Argument

It might seem that introducing Wittgenstein's resistance to the "new object" language weakens the trilogy's constitutive claim. In fact, it strengthens the argument for AI, for two reasons.

First, the Wittgensteinian version is more defensible. The Cassirerian claim that notation constitutes new objects carries an ontological commitment — to mathematical objects with a determinate structure, brought into being by the notation — that many philosophers will resist. The Wittgensteinian claim that proof institutes new rules carries no such commitment. It is a claim about practices and norms, not about objects and their mode of existence. If the AI argument can be made on the Wittgensteinian basis, it is philosophically lighter and harder to dismiss.

Second, the Wittgensteinian version is more directly testable. The question "does the system possess the object?" is difficult to operationalize. The question "does the system participate in the practice?" is less so: it asks whether the system recognizes and applies the norms of the practice, not merely whether it produces the patterns of its discourse. The reasoning-compute trend can be read as testing exactly this — and the asymptotic pattern, if it holds, is evidence that pattern-matching and practice-participation are genuinely different, that learning the textual traces of a practice is not the same as entering it.

This is the relocation the essay's title promises. Wittgenstein does not deny that something new comes into being with the notation. He denies that the new thing is an object. What comes into being is a rule — a norm, a criterion for correctness. And this normative reading of the constitutive claim is, for the purposes of the AI argument, both more defensible and more useful than the ontological one. The machine that would think must first learn to symbolize — but to symbolize, on the Wittgensteinian reading, is not to constitute objects in a formal medium. It is to enter a practice, to submit to its norms, and to recognize the difference between following a rule and merely matching a pattern.

## 8. Conclusion: The Asymptote, the Practice, and the Loop

The trilogy's argument can be restated in Wittgensteinian terms without loss, and with gain.

Intelligence is not underwritten by a single innate Mentalese but by the capacity to enter and coordinate multiple normative practices — language-games, in Wittgenstein's terms, or symbolic forms, in Cassirer's. Some of these practices — arithmetic, logic, proof, calculation — are constitutive in the sense that they create new norms, new criteria for what counts as correct, not merely new notations for pre-existing thoughts. A system trained only on the textual traces of these practices can learn their patterns, and the patterns carry it a long way. But the patterns are not the practice, and the gap between them is asymptotic: it narrows with scale but does not close, because it is a difference in kind — between matching a pattern and following a rule — not in degree.

The reasoning-compute trend is the experiment that tests this. If the asymptote is real, we should see diminishing returns on formal tasks as language-only reasoning scales, with residual errors in the normatively loaded cases — novel constructions, long derivations, the places where pattern-matching fails and rule-following is required. The current evidence is consistent with this prediction, though not yet decisive.

The prescription — internal reasoning joined to external formal systems — survives both the Cassirerian and Wittgensteinian readings, because both converge on the conclusion that the linguistic medium alone is insufficient. They diverge on the reason: Cassirer says the objects are not in the medium; Wittgenstein says the practice is not in the text. But the practical consequence is the same: to do proof, not merely talk about it, the system must enter the practice, and entering the practice requires more than scaling the discourse.

The loop — interpret, formalize, derive, verify, reinterpret — is the operational form of this conclusion. The model reasons in language about the problem; the formal system enforces the norms of the practice; the result returns to language for interpretation and revision. The model does not need to possess the objects, on the Cassirerian reading, or fully participate in the practice, on the Wittgensteinian. It needs to recognize when the linguistic medium is insufficient and hand off to the system that is sufficient. This is the computational form of the Wittgensteinian insight: the language-game of proof is not the language-game of conversation, and intelligence consists partly in knowing which game you are playing and when to stop playing it.

The asymptote, on this account, is not a failure of scale. It is the signature of a difference in kind — between discourse and practice, between pattern and rule, between talking about proof and proving. The machine that would cross it must do what Descartes did: not reason longer in the medium it has, but enter the medium it needs.
