# The Geometric Mind: Space, Time, and the Source of Exactness

*Dagfinn D. Dybvig with Mistral Vibe*

Working paper | September 2026

---

**Central thesis.** The capacity for exact reasoning may derive not from an internal symbolic engine but from the structure of the medium in which cognition operates. Space and time, as forms of intuition, supply a kind of necessity that is neither manufactured by a logic circuit nor extracted from statistical association. If so, the brain's exactness is geometric in origin, and the algebraic machinery Marcus posits is a learned formalization of a capacity that was spatial all along. This third path dissolves the opposition between the algebraic machine and the associative network by locating exactness in the form of representation rather than in a computational primitive.

---

## 1. Introduction: The Third Path

The preceding essay in this series brought Gary Marcus's *The Algebraic Mind* into conversation with the ecology of symbolic forms. Marcus asks what computational organization supports operations over variables and structured representations, and whether connectionist models can supply it. The essay accepted the question while resisting the nativist answer: the capacity to operate over variables may be learnable, and the symbolic languages that exploit it are certainly acquired. It distinguished three levels — enabling resources, learned structures, and public standards — and left the origin of the enabling resources open.

This essay takes up that open question. It argues that there is a third path between the two positions the debate has assumed. The first is the algebraic machine: a dedicated, possibly innate mechanism for variable binding and symbolic manipulation, implemented in neural tissue in a way that resembles a von Neumann processor. The second is the associative network: a system that learns statistical regularities and approximates rule-governed behavior without ever implementing rules. The third path, which this essay develops, is that the brain's exact reasoning is grounded in spatio-temporal modeling — the capacity to construct and manipulate representations in a medium whose structure supplies necessity directly.

The argument is Kantian in its philosophical ancestry and evolutionary in its motivation. Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781/1787) located the source of necessity in the forms of intuition — space and time — not in a logical engine or in empirical regularity. Constructions in pure intuition are exact not because a rule has been applied but because the medium in which they are constructed has a structure that makes certain relations necessary. The evolutionary argument supports this: spatial navigation, temporal prediction, and motor control are ancient capacities under continuous selective pressure, whereas a dedicated propositional logic engine has no comparable evolutionary pedigree. The capacity for exact reasoning may be a free rider on a spatio-temporal modeling capacity that evolved for navigation and prediction, not a separate faculty that evolved for its own sake.

This position has consequences for the architecture of artificial intelligence. If the brain's exactness is geometric, then the LLM — a sequence model with no inherent spatial structure — may be missing not a logic engine but a medium. No amount of training in symbolic systems will produce genuine algebraic competence in a system that lacks the representational medium in which exactness arises. Tools would be a permanent necessity, not a scaffolding for internalization. But the position also suggests what a better architecture would look like: not a symbolic engine bolted onto a neural network, but a system with genuine spatio-temporal modeling capacity, where the structure of the medium supplies the exactness that the symbolic layer would otherwise have to manufacture.

The essay proceeds in eight sections. Section 2 states the problem the third path addresses: why the algebraic machine and the associative network, despite their differences, share an assumption that the third path rejects. Section 3 develops the Kantian account of exactness from the medium of intuition. Section 4 makes the evolutionary argument. Section 5 connects the position to the series' Descartes essay: coordinate geometry as the formalization of a capacity that was spatial all along. Section 6 addresses the scope question: how much of mathematics and logic can be reconstructed from spatio-temporal constructions, and what handles the remainder. Section 7 draws the consequence for AI architecture. Section 8 concludes.

## 2. The Shared Assumption

The algebraic machine and the associative network disagree about mechanism but agree about the terms of the question. Both assume that exact reasoning is symbolic and that the question is whether the brain implements a symbolic engine. Marcus says it must, because systematic generalization requires operations over variables, and operations over variables require a mechanism that preserves binding and structure. The connectionist says it need not, because the relevant behavior can be approximated by statistical learning, and the approximation may be good enough. The disagreement is about whether the symbolic engine exists, not about what exact reasoning is.

This shared assumption is what the third path rejects. Exact reasoning need not be symbolic in its implementation. It need not involve a mechanism that manipulates discrete symbols according to explicit rules. The exactness of a geometric construction — the necessity that the interior angles of a triangle sum to two right angles — does not come from applying a rule to symbols. It comes from the structure of the space in which the construction is performed. The construction is exact because the medium is exact, not because a computation has been executed correctly.

This is not a claim that symbolic reasoning is impossible or unimportant. It is a claim about where the exactness comes from. A formal system like algebra or logic is exact, but its exactness may be inherited from the spatial and temporal intuitions that underwrite its constructions, not manufactured by its symbolic machinery. The symbols are a medium of expression; the necessity is supplied by something else. The third path asks what that something else is.

The question is not merely philosophical. It determines what you would look for in the brain and what you would build in silicon. If exactness comes from a symbolic engine, you look for binding mechanisms, variable representations, and rule-application circuits. If it comes from statistical association, you look for learning algorithms and training procedures. If it comes from the structure of the medium, you look for spatio-temporal modeling capacities — the brain's ability to construct, maintain, and manipulate spatial and temporal representations whose structure guarantees certain relations. The three positions predict different neural signatures and prescribe different architectures.

## 3. Kant's Insight: Necessity from the Medium

Kant's transcendental aesthetic locates space and time as the forms of sensible intuition. They are not objects of experience but the conditions under which experience is possible. Whatever appears to us appears in space and time, not because space and time are properties of things-in-themselves, but because they are the structure of the faculty through which we receive appearances. This is the Copernican turn: the object conforms to the faculty of representation, not the faculty to the object.

The crucial consequence for this essay is that space and time supply a kind of necessity that is prior to both empirical regularity and logical deduction. The truths of geometry are necessary not because they are analytic — Kant insisted they are synthetic a priori — and not because they are learned from experience. They are necessary because they are determined by the form of spatial intuition itself. To construct a triangle in pure intuition is to construct it in a medium whose structure makes certain properties unavoidable. The construction does not discover that the angles sum to two right angles; it exhibits the necessity of that sum in the very act of constructing.

This is the source of exactness the third path proposes. The exactness of geometric reasoning is not manufactured by a rule applied to symbols. It is inherited from the medium. The space in which the construction is performed has a structure, and that structure constrains what can be constructed in it. The necessity is in the medium, not in the operation. A system that operates in a medium with the right structure gets exactness for free; a system that operates in a medium without that structure would have to manufacture exactness some other way — by a symbolic engine, for instance.

The temporal form supplies a parallel kind of necessity. The succession of moments is not learned from experience; it is the form in which experience is had. Arithmetic, on Kant's account, depends on the successive synthesis of units in time. The necessity of `7 + 5 = 12` is not analytic — the concept of twelve is not contained in the concepts of seven and five — but synthetic a priori, grounded in the temporal construction of number. Whether this account of arithmetic succeeds is a question this essay will press in Section 6. The structural point is that temporal intuition, like spatial intuition, supplies necessity from the form of the medium rather than from a rule.

The transcendental aesthetic is a philosophical argument, not a neuroscientific theory. It tells us that exact spatial and temporal reasoning is possible and that its possibility depends on the form of intuition. It does not tell us what neural mechanism implements the form of intuition. Moving from "experience presupposes spatial and temporal form" to "the brain implements a spatio-temporal model whose structure produces exactness" is a philosophical leap, not an inference. The transcendental argument establishes a condition of possibility; it does not identify a mechanism. This essay proposes the spatio-temporal model as a candidate mechanism, not as a consequence of Kant's argument. The Kantian ancestry is philosophical motivation, not proof.

What the Kantian framework does supply is a way of thinking about exactness that the two standard positions lack. The algebraic machine treats exactness as a product of computation: the engine applies rules to symbols, and the correctness of the application guarantees the result. The associative network treats exactness as a product of learning: the system approximates the behavior, and the approximation is good enough for practical purposes. The Kantian position treats exactness as a product of the medium: the structure of the representational space guarantees certain relations, and the system that operates in that space inherits the guarantee. These are three different sources of necessity, and they prescribe three different architectures.

## 4. The Evolutionary Argument

The philosophical motivation is strengthened by an evolutionary consideration. Spatial navigation, temporal prediction, and motor control are ancient capacities. They are present in insects, fish, birds, and mammals. They are under continuous selective pressure: an organism that cannot model where it is, where predators are, and when to act does not survive. The neural machinery for spatial and temporal modeling is therefore likely to be deep, robust, and well-developed long before the emergence of language or formal reasoning.

A dedicated propositional logic engine has no comparable evolutionary pressure. What in ancestral environments selected for the ability to do modus ponens as such? The capacity for logical inference may be useful, but it is not obvious that it is useful in the specific, rule-governed, discrete form that a logic engine would implement. The ancestral environment selected for fast, reliable decision-making under uncertainty, not for valid inference over explicit premises. If logical competence is a separate faculty, it evolved without clear selective pressure for its specific form.

The spatio-temporal hypothesis avoids this difficulty. The capacity for exact spatial and temporal reasoning has obvious survival value independent of its later mathematical uses. An organism that can model the geometry of its environment — the angles of approach, the distances between landmarks, the trajectories of moving objects — has a direct advantage. If this capacity happens to support exact constructions that can later be formalized as geometry, the exactness is a free rider on a capacity that evolved for navigation. The mathematical uses are culturally developed extensions of a biological capacity that was selected for other reasons.

This is more parsimonious than the logic-engine hypothesis. It requires one ancient, well-motivated capacity rather than two — the spatial modeling capacity and the logical capacity. It explains why geometric reasoning feels natural and intuitive in a way that abstract logical reasoning does not: the geometric capacity is older, more practiced, and more directly connected to perception and action. And it explains why algebra, which is less directly spatial, feels harder: it requires the formalization of the spatial capacity into a new medium, a cultural achievement rather than a biological given.

The cerebellum provides a concrete neuroscientific anchor for this argument. It does precise, reliable, near-deterministic computation — timing, motor sequences, exact corrections — through a remarkably regular, repetitive circuit architecture. It is neural, and it is arguably the closest thing in the brain to a deterministic computational machine. But it does signal processing, not logic or algebra. The question is whether the same architectural strategy — regular, precise, repetitive circuitry operating over a structured input space — could implement the kind of spatio-temporal modeling that supports exact reasoning. The cerebellum suggests that neural tissue can achieve deterministic reliability when the circuit architecture is right. It does not show that this reliability extends to symbolic operations, but it shows that the "neural tissue cannot be deterministic" objection is not decisive. The determinism is an emergent property of the circuit, not a primitive of the neuron.

The hippocampus provides a second anchor. Place cells, grid cells, and head-direction cells implement a spatial map of the environment. The organism's location is represented as a position in a structured space, and the structure of the space constrains what positions are possible and what transitions between them are admissible. This is not a symbolic engine, but it is a system that represents and manipulates structured spatial information. If the hippocampal spatial map is the biological substrate of the Kantian form of spatial intuition, then the exactness of geometric reasoning may be inherited from the structure of the map, not manufactured by a separate logical faculty. The map is the medium; the necessity is in the medium.

The evolutionary argument does not prove the hypothesis. It makes it plausible. The logic-engine hypothesis has no comparable evolutionary motivation. The associationist hypothesis has evolutionary plausibility but cannot explain the exactness. The spatio-temporal hypothesis has evolutionary plausibility and a candidate source of exactness. It is the only position that addresses both constraints.

## 5. Descartes Revisited: Algebra as Formalized Geometry

The series' Descartes essay, *The Cartesian Moment*, argued that analytical geometry was not merely a technical advance but the creation of a new symbolic form — a language of thought that made spatial extension formally tractable. The coordinate system translated geometric objects into algebraic equations, and the equations could be manipulated by syntactic rules without reference to spatial intuition. This was the constitutive move: the notation created new objects — functions, derivatives, integrals — that were not available in classical geometry or natural language.

The spatio-temporal hypothesis adds a layer to this account. If the brain's exact reasoning is fundamentally spatial, then Descartes' achievement takes on a different significance. Coordinate geometry is not the creation of a new capacity from nothing. It is the formalization of a capacity that was spatial all along. The bridge between geometric intuition and algebraic manipulation is not a bridge between two unrelated domains; it is the externalization of a capacity that the brain already possessed in spatial form. Algebra becomes a cultural formalization of spatial reasoning, not a separate symbolic capacity that the brain had to implement independently.

This reframes the constitutive thesis. *The Cartesian Moment* argued that the symbolic form constitutes new objects — the derivative, the function, the integral — that were not available before the notation existed. The spatio-temporal hypothesis agrees but adds: the capacity to understand and use these objects is grounded in a spatial modeling capacity that predates the notation. The notation does not create the capacity; it makes the capacity tractable in a new medium. The derivative is a new object, constituted by the calculus, but the spatial intuition of rate of change — the sense that something is increasing or decreasing at a certain pace — is available to any organism that can model trajectories. The calculus formalizes this intuition; it does not create it from nothing.

This is consistent with the series' distinction between enabling resources and learned structures. The spatial modeling capacity is the enabling resource. The coordinate system, the calculus, and the algebraic notation are learned structures. The enabling resource does not specify which structures will be invented or what objects they will constitute. It supplies the capacity to understand and use them once they are invented. The learned structures are transformative — they make new thoughts possible — but they are transformative because they formalize and extend a capacity that was already there, not because they create a capacity that was absent.

The connection to Cassirer is direct. In *Substance and Function*, Cassirer argued that scientific concepts are not collections of common properties abstracted from individual things but relational systems that organize knowledge. The function is not a property of a thing; it is a relation among variables. The spatio-temporal hypothesis supplies a candidate for the enabling resource that makes Cassirer's relational systems learnable: the brain's capacity to model spatial and temporal relations. Marcus's variables offer a computational resource for preserving relations as their occupants change; the spatio-temporal model offers a medium in which relations are already present, because the medium is relational. The variables are a formalization of something the medium already does.

This also clarifies why the series' constitutive claim does not collapse into nativism. The capacity for spatial modeling may be innate — it is evolutionarily ancient and neuroscientifically grounded — but the symbolic forms that formalize it are culturally acquired. Coordinate geometry is not innate; the spatial capacity it formalizes is. The derivative is not innate; the temporal intuition of rate that it formalizes is. The three-level distinction from the Algebraic Mind essay holds: enabling resources (spatio-temporal modeling), learned structures (coordinate geometry, calculus, algebra), and public standards (proof, measurement, peer criticism). The nativism is at level one, and it is a nativism about a modeling capacity, not about a symbolic engine.

## 6. The Scope Question: How Much Can Geometry Carry?

The spatio-temporal hypothesis faces a direct challenge. Geometry, topology, the spatial aspects of physics — these fit naturally. But abstract algebra, set theory, category theory, and propositional logic itself have no obvious spatial construction. If spatio-temporal modeling grounds some exact reasoning but not all, then the hypothesis explains the geometric part and pushes the problem to the non-geometric remainder.

Kant himself was aware of this difficulty. His account of arithmetic as the successive synthesis of units in time is a temporal construction, but it is strained. The natural numbers can be generated by successive addition, but the account does not obviously generalize to negative numbers, rational numbers, real numbers, or complex numbers. The axiom of choice, the continuum hypothesis, and the machinery of transfinite arithmetic have no intuitive temporal construction. Kant's account of arithmetic works for counting and for elementary operations; it does not work for the full edifice of modern number theory.

The challenge is sharper for logic. Propositional logic, first-order logic, and modal logic are not spatial or temporal in any obvious sense. Modus ponens is not a construction in space or time; it is a rule of inference. If the brain's exactness is geometric, what makes logical inference exact? The spatio-temporal hypothesis needs an answer, or it must concede that logic falls outside its scope.

Three responses are available, in increasing order of ambition.

The first and most modest is to accept the limitation. The spatio-temporal hypothesis explains the exactness of geometric and spatio-temporal reasoning. Logic and abstract algebra are cultural formalizations that are learned and used as symbolic systems, supported by the enabling resources of memory, attention, and structured representation that the Algebraic Mind essay identified. On this reading, the brain has two sources of exactness: the spatio-temporal medium for geometric reasoning, and the learned symbolic practices for logical reasoning. The symbolic practices are exact not because the brain implements a logic engine but because they are public, norm-governed systems whose exactness is enforced externally — by proof checkers, by peer review, by the community of practitioners. This is the Wittgensteinian move: exactness is in the practice, not in the brain. The brain's contribution is the capacity to learn and follow the practice, not a mechanism that manufactures exactness internally.

This response is defensible but leaves the logical remainder unexplained. It says where the exactness of logic is not — not in a brain-based logic engine — but it does not say where it is. The Wittgensteinian answer — in the practice — is a claim about normativity, not about mechanism. It explains why logical conclusions are correct; it does not explain how the brain produces them.

The second response is to extend the spatio-temporal account to logic by way of structural analogy. Logical inference can be represented spatially: Venn diagrams for syllogistic logic, truth tables as spatial arrays, proof trees as directed graphs. These representations are not merely pedagogical devices; they may exploit the same spatial modeling capacity that geometric reasoning uses. The necessity of a valid syllogism can be seen in a Venn diagram: the conclusion is forced by the spatial arrangement of the regions. If the spatial representation is not a crutch but the medium in which the inference is actually performed, then logical exactness is inherited from the spatial medium, just as geometric exactness is. The formal notation of logic — the symbols, the connectives, the rules — is a cultural formalization of a capacity that is spatial at its base.

This response is more ambitious and more contestable. It requires that the spatial representations of logic are not merely illustrations of something that is really happening in a different medium, but are the medium in which the reasoning occurs. The evidence for this is indirect: people find spatial representations of logical relations intuitive and helpful, and the brain's spatial modeling systems are active during logical reasoning tasks. But the evidence does not settle whether the spatial representation is the reasoning or a support for reasoning that occurs elsewhere.

The third response is the most ambitious: to claim that all exact reasoning, including logic and abstract algebra, is ultimately grounded in spatio-temporal structure, even when the spatial component is not visible. On this reading, the formal systems that seem non-spatial — algebra, set theory, category theory — are spatial at a deeper level, in the way that the surface grammar of a language may not reveal its underlying structure. The variables of algebra are positions in a structured space; the operations are transformations of that space; the necessity of the results is inherited from the space's structure. This is a strong claim, and it echoes a tradition in the philosophy of mathematics that runs from Kant through Riemann and Helmholtz to the modern geometric foundations of algebra (Grothendieck's schemes, the spatial intuition behind topos theory). It is not a claim this essay can establish, but it is a direction the hypothesis suggests.

The three responses are not exclusive. The spatio-temporal hypothesis may explain the exactness of geometric reasoning directly, the exactness of logic through structural analogy, and the exactness of abstract algebra through deeper spatial structure that is not immediately visible. The scope of the hypothesis depends on how far the spatial grounding extends, and that is an empirical and mathematical question, not a philosophical one. What the hypothesis does establish is that the spatial grounding is available as a source of exactness, and that it is a source the two standard positions do not consider.

## 7. The Architecture Question

If the brain's exactness is geometric, the consequences for AI architecture are specific and consequential.

The current approach in this series — LLM foundational, surrounded by tools, trained in symbolic systems — is a pragmatic response to the absence of a strong neurosymbolic engine. The LLM supplies interpretation, natural language, and broad domain coverage. The tools supply reliable execution within formal systems. The training in symbolic systems is meant to develop the LLM's competence in formalization — knowing what to formalize and how — so that the tools are used correctly. The architecture's reliability is bounded by the tools' verifiability, which is a feature: it keeps the system honest.

The spatio-temporal hypothesis does not refute this architecture, but it changes the diagnosis of its limitations. The Algebraic Mind essay identified the formalization gap as the architecture's vulnerability: the tools verify the derivation but not the formalization, and the LLM is the component doing the formalization. The spatio-temporal hypothesis adds a reason why the formalization gap may be hard to close. If the LLM's exactness is limited by the absence of a spatial medium, then training in symbolic systems may improve its ability to manipulate symbols but not its ability to construct the spatial representations that underwrite exact formalization. The LLM can learn the patterns of algebraic manipulation without possessing the spatial intuition that makes those manipulations meaningful. It can produce correct formalizations in familiar cases — where the spatial structure is implicit in the training data — and fail in novel cases — where the spatial structure would have to be constructed from scratch.

This suggests that the formalization gap is not merely a training problem but a medium problem. The LLM operates in a linguistic medium. Language can describe spatial relations, but describing a spatial relation is not the same as operating in a spatial medium. The description can be correct; the operation may not be available. If the exactness of formalization depends on the ability to construct spatial representations, and the LLM's medium does not support spatial construction, then no amount of training in symbolic systems will produce the capacity. The tools are not scaffolding for internalization; they are a permanent substitute for a capacity the medium cannot supply.

But the hypothesis also suggests what a better architecture would look like. If the brain's exactness comes from spatio-temporal modeling, then the architecture that would replicate it is not a symbolic engine bolted onto a neural network. It is a system with genuine spatio-temporal modeling capacity — a system that can construct, maintain, and manipulate representations in a medium whose structure supplies necessity. This is closer to the active inference framework that Marcus has been engaging with than to the neurosymbolic program as currently practiced. Active inference, in Karl Friston's formulation, posits systems that maintain generative models of their environment and act to minimize prediction error. The model is spatio-temporal: it represents the environment as a structured space of possible states and the organism's actions as trajectories through that space. If the structure of the model supplies the exactness — if the necessity of certain relations is inherited from the geometry of the state space — then active inference offers a candidate architecture for the third path.

This is not a claim that active inference is the right architecture. It is a claim that the spatio-temporal hypothesis points toward a class of architectures that neither the symbolic-engine nor the bolt-on approach belongs to. These architectures would have spatial structure as a primitive, not as a learned feature. They would represent relations in a medium whose structure guarantees certain necessities. They would not need a symbolic engine because the medium supplies the exactness that the engine would otherwise manufacture. And they would not need external tools for the same reason — though tools would still be valuable for verification, for extending the range of tractable problems, and for accountability.

The relationship to Marcus's program is nuanced. Marcus advocates neurosymbolic AI — combining neural networks with symbolic reasoning. The spatio-temporal hypothesis is compatible with this in a broad sense: a system with spatial modeling capacity plus symbolic tools is neurosymbolic. But it differs from Marcus's vision in where the exactness resides. Marcus's neurosymbolic AI puts the exactness in the symbolic component. The spatio-temporal hypothesis puts the exactness in the spatial medium, with the symbolic component as a formalization of what the medium already does. The difference matters for engineering: Marcus would build a better symbolic engine; the spatio-temporal hypothesis would build a better spatial model.

The relationship to the series' existing architecture is also nuanced. The LLM-plus-tools approach is a pragmatic response to the current state of technology. It works because the tools supply the exactness that the LLM lacks. The spatio-temporal hypothesis says that the LLM lacks the exactness not because it lacks a symbolic engine but because it lacks a spatial medium. This is a stronger claim about the limitation, and it has a more specific prescription: the path forward is not better tools or more training but a different representational medium. Whether that medium can be built is the open question — and it is the same question that the strong neurosymbolic program faces. Nobody has built a neural system with genuine spatial modeling capacity that supports exact reasoning. The cerebellum and hippocampus suggest it is possible in biological tissue. Whether it is possible in silicon is the engineering question that would settle the philosophical debate.

## 8. Conclusion: Three Sources of Necessity

The essay has distinguished three sources of necessity for exact reasoning, each prescribing a different architecture.

The first is the symbolic engine. Exactness comes from the correct application of rules to discrete symbols. The architecture is a logic machine, possibly implemented in neural tissue. This is Marcus's position in its strongest form. Its weakness is that nobody has found such an engine in the brain, and the evolutionary pressure for a dedicated propositional logic faculty is unclear. The strong neurosymbolic program — building a neural system that implements deterministic symbolic logic — is the engineering expression of this position. It has not succeeded, and the failure is more likely technical than political.

The second is statistical association. Exactness is an illusion; what exists is reliable approximation. The architecture is a sufficiently large neural network trained on sufficient data. This is the pure scaling position. Its weakness is that it cannot explain the necessity of exact reasoning — the sense in which `7 + 5 = 12` is not merely highly probable but necessary — and the empirical evidence of diminishing returns from pure scaling is growing.

The third is the medium. Exactness comes from the structure of the representational space in which reasoning is performed. The architecture is a system with genuine spatio-temporal modeling capacity, where the geometry of the state space supplies the necessity that the other positions must manufacture or approximate. This is the Kantian position, motivated by evolutionary plausibility and supported by neuroscientific evidence from the cerebellum and hippocampus. Its weakness is that the scope is uncertain — it is unclear how much of mathematics and logic can be grounded in spatio-temporal structure — and the mechanism is unspecified. The transcendental argument establishes a condition of possibility; it does not identify a neural mechanism.

The series' pragmatic architecture — LLM foundational, tools for verification, training in symbolic systems — is a response to the current state of technology. It does not commit to any of the three positions. It works regardless of which source of necessity is correct, because the tools supply the exactness externally. But the diagnosis of its limitations depends on which position is right. If the symbolic engine is the source, the limitation is that the LLM lacks the engine, and the tools are a substitute for it. If association is the source, there is no fundamental limitation, only insufficient scale. If the medium is the source, the limitation is that the LLM lacks the spatial medium, and the tools are a substitute for a capacity that training cannot supply.

The author's philosophical commitment, stated in the conversations that produced this essay, is to the third path. The difficulty of conceiving a genuine algebraic machine inside the brain — a neural structure that implements deterministic propositional logic — is not merely pragmatic but philosophical. Every neural mechanism is continuous at the level of individual neurons. The determinism of a symbolic engine would have to be an emergent stability, not a primitive, and it is unclear what circuit architecture would produce it for logical operations specifically. The spatio-temporal hypothesis avoids this difficulty by locating exactness in the medium rather than in a mechanism. The medium is continuous too, but its structure — the geometry of the space — supplies necessity without requiring discrete symbolic operations. The exactness is geometric, not logical.

This commitment is compatible with openness to revision. If someone were to find a neural structure that implements logic and algebra in a deterministic way — the "find" path — or to build one in silicon — the "make" path — the author would accept it. The philosophical skepticism is about the existence of such a structure in the brain, not about the coherence of the concept. Strong neurosymbolic AI, in the sense of neural systems that implement deterministic symbolic logic, is a legitimate research program. The essay's claim is that it is not the only path, and that the spatio-temporal path is philosophically more plausible and evolutionarily better motivated.

The settlement, if it comes, will not be philosophical. It will be empirical. Someone will either find a neural mechanism that does deterministic symbolic logic without associative machinery, or build one in silicon, or find a neural mechanism whose spatio-temporal structure explains a class of exact reasoning that associationist models cannot reproduce. The first would vindicate Marcus. The third would vindicate the Kantian path. The second could vindicate either, depending on what was built. Until one of these happens, the three positions remain open, and the pragmatic architecture — LLM plus tools plus training — remains the rational response to uncertainty.

The mind described in this essay is geometric in its capacity for exactness and plural in the symbolic forms that formalize that capacity. Its education is not the accumulation of a logic engine but the cultivation of a medium. Marcus asks how a learner can carry a rule beyond the cases it has seen. The Kantian answer is: by constructing the rule in a medium whose structure makes the construction necessary. The preceding essays ask how such rules become instruments of thought. The answer remains: through the cultural invention of symbolic forms that formalize the medium's capacity and make it tractable in new ways. The geometry is old. The algebra is learned. The task is to build a machine that has the geometry, not one that merely talks about it.
