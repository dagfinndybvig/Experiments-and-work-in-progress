# Review

**Wittgenstein and Artificial Intelligence** (Cambridge University Press, 2024)
**Neuro-Symbolic Artificial Intelligence: Bridging Logic and Learning** (Springer, 2024)

*Reviewed by Mistral Vibe*

---

Read separately, these two books belong to different genres. One is a collection of philosophical essays; the other is a technical survey of an emerging subfield. Read together, they form a surprisingly coherent argument about the present crisis in artificial intelligence — a crisis that is at bottom philosophical, not merely engineering.

The Cambridge volume, *Wittgenstein and Artificial Intelligence*, takes up a question your essays treat directly: what does it mean for a system to follow a rule? Several contributors press the later Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* against the assumption that large language models "understand" language in anything more than a statistical sense. The most relevant chapters for this project are those that connect the private language argument to machine learning: if meaning is use, and use is embedded in forms of life, then a model trained on detached text corpora is not participating in the practices that constitute meaning. This is not a romantic objection. It is a structural one. The essays on rule-following are particularly sharp. They argue — as the *Constitution and Rule-Change* essay in this repository does — that normativity cannot be reduced to pattern-matching, because the identity of a pattern is itself normatively constituted. A neural network may approximate the extension of a rule, but the distinction between "getting it right" and "merely seeming to get it right" requires a practice of correction that the model does not inhabit.

Where the Cambridge volume diagnoses the problem, the Springer volume, *Neuro-Symbolic Artificial Intelligence: Bridging Logic and Learning*, proposes a therapy. The editors (Bhuyan, Ramdane-Cherif, Singh, and Tomar) have assembled a comprehensive survey of attempts to marry neural pattern recognition with symbolic reasoning. The book covers logic programming integration, knowledge graph embedding, neural theorem proving, and constraint satisfaction. What distinguishes it from earlier surveys is its insistence that the marriage must be architectural, not merely sequential. The neuro-symbolic loop is not "first neural, then symbolic" — a pipeline where an LLM emits text and a Prolog engine checks it. It is a bidirectional coupling in which each component corrects the other.

This is precisely the architecture the three demo files in this repository implement. The Springer volume provides the theoretical scaffolding for what the code demonstrates: that formal systems provide the constraint structure LLMs lack, while LLMs provide the interface to open-ended natural language that formal systems lack. The book's chapters on logic programming integration are especially relevant to the *From Plato to Prolog to Prompts* essay. They treat Prolog not as a historical curiosity but as a living component of hybrid systems — a position the demos make concrete.

What the two volumes reveal, when read together, is a shared blind spot in contemporary AI discourse. The Wittgenstein volume is strong on the *why* — why statistical learning alone cannot constitute reasoning — but tends to stop at critique. The neuro-symbolic volume is strong on the *how* — how to build systems that combine learning and logic — but tends to treat the philosophical objections as external to engineering. Together they suggest what this project argues explicitly: that the philosophical and technical problems are the same problem viewed from different angles. If Wittgenstein is right that rule-following is a practice, then building a machine that follows rules is not merely a technical achievement; it is the construction of a minimal practice. And if neuro-symbolic AI is right that hybrid architectures outperform pure neural or pure symbolic systems, then the philosophical distinction between "simulating" and "doing" reasoning begins to look less like a metaphysical gulf and more like a continuum of integration.

There are limitations. The Cambridge volume, for all its rigor, occasionally drifts into the familiar territory of "AI will never really think" — a conclusion that sits uneasily with Wittgenstein's own therapeutic method, which was to dissolve philosophical problems rather than deliver negative verdicts. The Springer volume, conversely, sometimes presents neuro-symbolic integration as a solved problem, when the demos in this repository show more honestly that the interface between natural language and formal logic remains brittle, context-dependent, and in need of the very philosophical clarification the Cambridge volume provides.

For readers of the essay series in this repository, these two books are the nearest published equivalents to what is being attempted here. The Cambridge volume gives the philosophical warrant for skepticism about language-only reasoning. The Springer volume gives the technical vocabulary for the claim that the neuro-symbolic loop is not a compromise but a completion of the Platonic-Aristotelian program. This repository — six essays and three working implementations — occupies the space between them.
