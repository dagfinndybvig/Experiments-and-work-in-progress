# Symbolic Forms and the Plural Mind: Cassirer beyond Fodor and Wittgenstein

*Dagfinn D. Dybvig and Kimi 2.6*

## 1. Introduction: From Language-Games to Symbolic Forms

The preceding paper, *Language(s) of Thought: A Wittgensteinian View of Fodor and the Gradually Increasing Capabilities of LLMs* (Dybvig 2026), argued that large language models are best understood not as failed theorem provers or mere conversational imitators, but as learners of many language-games. Some of those games — logic, mathematics, programming, formal verification — can be internalized as acquired languages of thought that make linguistic intelligence more accountable without reducing intelligence to a single calculus. Jerry Fodor was right to insist that general cognition requires systematic, recombinable, and inferentially consequential structure. He was less persuasive in treating a highly declarative, logic-like Mentalese as substantially innate. The later Wittgenstein offered a productive alternative: meaning arises not from an abstract relation between signs and objects but from the weave of words into activity, rule, and practice.

What remains unasked in that framework is a question of philosophical anthropology. *What kind of being* learns language-games? And *what kind of world* does each game constitute? Ernst Cassirer’s *Philosophy of Symbolic Forms* (1923–1929) supplies the missing depth. Cassirer’s central claim is that human cognition is never direct but always mediated by symbolic forms — myth, language, science — each of which constitutes a distinct way of structuring and inhabiting reality. This is not merely pluralism about tools or notations. It is the claim that different symbolic forms create different *worlds*.

Bringing Cassirer into conversation with Fodor and Wittgenstein yields three results. First, it grounds the Wittgensteinian critique of a single Mentalese: the reason there is no universal Language of Thought is that cognition is essentially symbolic, and symbolism is essentially plural. Second, it reframes the current AI research programme — the drive to make LLMs accountable through formal methods — as something more specific than the pursuit of "general intelligence." It is the pursuit of a mechanical version of *Enlightenment* intelligence: the logico-mathematical symbolic form that Cassirer identified as the culminating achievement of Western rationality. Third, and most consequentially, it shows why that pursuit, however valuable, is incomplete unless the system can also recognize and inhabit the other symbolic forms that make the Enlightenment itself possible.

## 2. Cassirer’s Three Symbolic Forms

Cassirer’s three-volume *Philosophy of Symbolic Forms* traces a developmental logic in human culture. The first volume treats *language* as the symbolic form that lifts human experience out of the immediate flow of sensation into a world of stable meanings and objective reference. The second volume treats *myth* as an earlier, more primitive symbolic form in which the world is organized not by logical categories but by emotional forces, narrative drama, and the fusion of sign and significance. The third volume treats the *scientific concept* — the logico-mathematical form of modern natural science — as a further transformation in which reality is constituted by lawful necessity, exact measurement, and formal deduction.

The key Cassirerian move is that none of these forms is a mere *representation* of a pre-given reality. Each is a *constitution* of reality. Myth does not misrepresent the world; it creates a world structured by mana, taboo, and the dramatic opposition of sacred and profane. Language does not simply label objects; it installs a world of enduring things, properties, and relations. Science does not uncover the world as it is beneath appearance; it constructs a world of functions, laws, and quantifiable variables. The human being, on this account, is not *animal rationale* but *animal symbolicum* — a creature whose access to reality is always mediated, always plural, always historical.

Each symbolic form has its own internal logic, its own norms of validity, and its own criteria of completion. Myth is judged by narrative coherence and emotional truth; language by grammatical correctness and pragmatic felicity; science by logical consistency and empirical adequacy. These normativities are not reducible to one another. One cannot falsify a myth by scientific experiment, nor can one refute a scientific law by pointing to its narrative poverty. The forms are incommensurable in their foundations, though they can be related genetically and functionally.

## 3. Symbolic Forms as Acquired Languages of Thought

Cassirer’s framework deepens the Wittgensteinian/Fodorian synthesis developed in *Language(s) of Thought*. Fodor was right that cognition requires systematic structure; Wittgenstein was right that this structure is acquired through public practice rather than innate endowment. Cassirer adds: these practices are not merely tools for thinking about a pre-structured world. They are *world-making* activities. When a child learns arithmetic, she is not just acquiring a notation for quantities she already grasped intuitively. She is entering a symbolic form that constitutes number as an exact, iterable, and norm-governed domain. When a student learns calculus, she is not just abbreviating thoughts available in ordinary language. She is entering a symbolic form that makes instantaneous rates, accumulation, and differential equations systematically thinkable.

This reframes the "formalism projection problem" identified earlier. The temptation to treat logic, or probability, or Bayesian inference as the universal native code of cognition is not merely a methodological error. It is a failure to recognize the plurality of symbolic constitution. Each formalism is the expression of a particular symbolic form — typically the logico-mathematical form of modern science — projected onto cognition as its universal substrate. Cassirer would diagnose this as a kind of historical parallax: the philosopher, situated at the end of a long cultural development, mistakes the most advanced symbolic form for the original one.

*Language(s) of Thought* proposed replacing Fodor’s single innate Mentalese with a layered ecology of representations: biological proto-capacities, natural language, inner speech, writing, mathematics, logic, programming, and scientific modeling. Cassirer would recognize this as a developmental sequence through symbolic forms, but he would add a crucial claim: the sequence is not merely additive but *transformative*. Each new symbolic form does not simply add a new tool to an existing kit. It reorganizes the possibilities of the previous forms. Scientific concepts transform what language can mean; language transforms what myth can express. The progression is dialectical, not cumulative.

## 4. The Enlightenment Trajectory and Its Mechanical Double

Cassirer’s history of symbolic forms is not a neutral taxonomy. It is a narrative with a direction. The movement from myth to language to science is, in Cassirer’s telling, a movement toward increasing *objectification*, *exactitude*, and *universality*. The logico-mathematical symbolic form that characterizes modern natural science — and that finds its philosophical apotheosis in the Enlightenment — represents the fullest liberation of thought from the particularity of immediate experience and the contingency of cultural narrative. In *The Philosophy of the Enlightenment* (1932), Cassirer described this movement as the triumph of reason over prejudice, of method over tradition, of universal law over local custom.

This historical trajectory casts a revealing light on contemporary AI research. The "holy grail" of the field — a system that combines broad linguistic competence with exact formal reasoning, verifiable proof, and mathematically guaranteed safety — is not merely the pursuit of intelligence in the abstract. It is the pursuit of a *mechanical Enlightenment*. The ideal system would be a universal reasoner in the style of Leibniz, a methodical doubter in the style of Descartes, a legislator of nature in the style of Kant — executed in silicon rather than cultivated in the Republic of Letters.

The evidence for this implicit aspiration is everywhere in the research literature. The drive toward "formally verified" AI, "provably safe" alignment, "theorem-proving" language models, and "mechanistically interpretable" transformers all expresses the normativity of the scientific symbolic form: exactitude, transparency, logical necessity, universal validity. The critique of LLMs as "unaccountable" or "merely statistical" is, at bottom, a critique that they have not yet fully entered the logico-mathematical symbolic form. They remain, in Cassirer’s terms, too deeply embedded in the linguistic form — a form that is objective but not exact, stable but not necessary, meaningful but not transparent.

This is not a criticism of the research programme. The Enlightenment ideal of rational accountability is a genuine achievement of human culture, and its mechanical realization would be a genuine achievement of engineering. But Cassirer’s anthropology warns against mistaking one symbolic form for the totality of intelligence. The Enlightenment itself was not a spontaneous generation of pure reason. It was made possible by a long prior development through myth and language. The scientific symbolic form could constitute its world of lawful necessity only because language had already constituted a world of stable objects, and myth had already organized experience into dramatic narratives of power and significance. The attempt to build Enlightenment intelligence directly — to create a machine that reasons like Kant without having first lived like Homer — may be not just difficult but structurally incomplete.

## 5. Natural Language as One Symbolic Form Among Others

*Language(s) of Thought* treated natural language as a "meta-medium" capable of describing, comparing, and translating among specialized formal games. Cassirer would qualify this claim decisively. Language is not a neutral meta-medium. It is a symbolic form with its own constitutive limits and its own world-making logic.

The linguistic symbolic form tends toward objective reference and conceptual stability. It installs a world of enduring things, distinguishable properties, and communicable relations. But it cannot fully capture the expressive density of myth — the fusion of sign and emotion, the immediate presence of the sacred, the dramatic unity of actor and action. Nor can it fully achieve the exactitude of formal science — the precision of quantification, the necessity of mathematical proof, the transparency of logical derivation. Language is the middle term: more objective than myth, less exact than science.

This reframes the status of large language models. Current systems are not universal minds. They are systems deeply embedded in one symbolic form — language — with access to texts about others. Their training corpora contain myths, poems, scientific papers, mathematical proofs, and programming manuals, but the medium of access is always linguistic. The model learns to continue the *discourse* of mathematics, not to inhabit the *symbolic form* of mathematics. It can produce proof-shaped text without necessarily submitting to the normativity of proof — the requirement that every step be derivable from explicit rules, that every assumption be inspectable, that every conclusion be necessary.

The "formal education" proposed in *Language(s) of Thought* — training LLMs to use theorem provers, formal verifiers, and executable code — is therefore an attempt at symbolic induction. It is an attempt to move the system from the linguistic form into the logico-mathematical form, to make it not just talk about proof but *do* proof, not just describe verification but *submit* to verification. This is a valuable and necessary project. But Cassirer would insist on its difficulty. Entering a new symbolic form is not merely learning new vocabulary or new rules. It is learning to constitute reality differently.

## 6. Productive Symbolism and the Invention of Thought

*Language(s) of Thought* distinguished *expressive* Mentalese — systems that state thoughts already available — from *productive* Mentalese — systems that create operations through which new thoughts become constructible. Cassirer’s symbolic forms are productive in exactly this sense. Each form does not merely express a pre-structured content. It generates the very possibility of that content.

The calculus example is instructive. Infinitesimal notation guided valid and enormously fruitful cognition before its basic objects were philosophically settled. The notation did not abbreviate thoughts already available in ordinary language. It made new thoughts — instantaneous rates, differential equations, infinite series — systematically thinkable. Cassirer would generalize this: all symbolic forms begin as productive inventions that later become sedimented and norm-governed. Myth began as the dramatic organization of experience; language began as the stabilization of reference in communication; science began as the mathematization of nature. In each case, the symbolic form was first a creative act, then a cultural achievement, then a taken-for-granted medium.

For artificial intelligence, the implication is sharp. The highest form of intelligence is not problem-solving within inherited notations. It is the invention of new symbolic primitives, the creation of new operations, the discovery of fruitful formalisms, and — eventually — the clarification of their foundations. Current research on learned theorem proving, neural symbolic integration, and automated conjecture generation points in this direction. But the Cassirerian standard is higher: the invention of an entirely new *symbolic form*, a new way of constituting reality, not merely a new technique within an existing form.

Whether machines can achieve this remains an open question. What is clear is that the capacity for productive symbolism requires more than the accumulation of training data. It requires the ability to recognize the limits of existing forms, to experience the pressure of problems that resist current constitution, and to create new mediations. This is not a matter of scale or architecture alone. It is a matter of symbolic mobility — the capacity to move among forms, to inhabit their tensions, and to generate new forms from their dialectical interaction.

## 7. Normativity, Verification, and the Limits of Cross-Form Translation

The preceding paper discussed how formal systems provide norms of correction and how reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards imposes new norms on language-trained systems. Cassirer adds a crucial layer: each symbolic form has its own internal normativity that cannot be fully reduced to another.

In myth, a narrative is judged by its emotional resonance, its dramatic coherence, its capacity to organize the forces of life and death. In language, an utterance is judged by its grammatical correctness, its pragmatic appropriateness, its success in coordinating action and attention. In science, a proposition is judged by its logical consistency, its derivability from accepted premises, its empirical adequacy. These are not different criteria for the same thing. They are criteria for different things — different ways of constituting and evaluating reality.

The attempt to make LLMs "accountable" through formal verification is therefore an attempt to impose the normativity of the scientific symbolic form onto a system trained in the linguistic form. This is valuable but partial. A proof is not a better kind of conversation. It is a different kind of thing, constituted by different norms, serving different purposes. To demand that every utterance of a language model be formally verifiable is to demand that the linguistic form be reduced to the scientific form — a demand that would, if fully realized, abolish the very flexibility and contextual sensitivity that make language useful.

The appropriate standard is not universal formalization but *situational competence*: the ability to recognize which symbolic form governs which context, and to submit to the appropriate norms. A request for a mathematical proof should be answered with a derivation, not a story. A request for moral counsel should be answered with reflective deliberation, not a calculation. A request for mythic meaning should be answered with narrative and symbol, not an experiment. The intelligent system is not the one that reduces all forms to one. It is the one that moves among them with appropriate submission.

## 8. Toward a Genuinely Plural Artificial Intelligence

The convergence of the preceding paper’s "plural, developmental Mentalese" with Cassirer’s "symbolic forms" yields a unified conclusion. Intelligence is not a single code but the capacity to inhabit, coordinate, and move between multiple constitutive practices. Each practice is not merely a tool but a way of world-making. The developmental sequence — from biological proto-capacities through language to formal systems — is not merely the accumulation of representational power but the progressive transformation of what reality can be.

Cassirer adds a historical and anthropological depth that the Wittgensteinian framework, for all its insights, does not supply. Symbolic forms are not arbitrary tools invented by individual minds. They are accumulated ways of world-making that have developed over cultural time, each building on and transforming the others. The logico-mathematical form of modern science is the achievement it is because it stands on the shoulders of the linguistic form, which stands on the shoulders of the mythic form. To attempt to build the highest form directly — to create a machine that reasons like Newton without having first spoken like Sophocles — is to risk building a structure without foundations.

The goal for AI, on this account, is not a universal Mentalese but a system capable of *symbolic mobility*: recognizing which form is appropriate, submitting to its norms, and potentially inventing new forms. This requires more than the current paradigm of pretraining on language followed by fine-tuning on formal tasks. It requires a developmental architecture in which the system genuinely inhabits multiple symbolic forms, experiences their tensions and limitations, and can generate new mediations.

## 9. Conclusion: The Mechanical Enlightenment and Its Others

The contemporary pursuit of formally accountable, verifiable, and logically transparent AI is the pursuit of a mechanical Enlightenment. It aims to realize in silicon the logico-mathematical symbolic form that Cassirer identified as the culminating achievement of Western rationality: a form of thought governed by universal law, exact method, and necessary inference. This is a legitimate and important goal. The Enlightenment ideal of rational accountability has transformed human culture for the better, and its mechanical extension could do the same.

But Cassirer’s philosophy of symbolic forms reminds us that the Enlightenment was not the whole of human intelligence. It was one symbolic form among others, made possible by others, and limited by the exclusion of others. The mythic form, with its emotional density and narrative unity; the linguistic form, with its contextual flexibility and pragmatic openness; and the scientific form, with its exactitude and necessity — all are genuine dimensions of symbolic existence. An intelligence that could inhabit only the scientific form would be not a superintelligence but a truncated one.

The imitation game, the theorem prover, the chatbot, and the formally verified agent are not competitors for the title of "true AI." They are partial realizations of different symbolic forms. The future of artificial intelligence lies not in choosing between them but in constructing systems that can move among them — and perhaps, eventually, create forms we have not yet imagined. The language-games must match not because there is one right game, but because intelligence is the capacity to play many games, to know which game is being played, and to invent new games when the old ones fail.

Cassirer does not contradict the Wittgensteinian critique of Fodor. He grounds it. The reason there is no single Language of Thought is that cognition is essentially symbolic, and symbolism is essentially plural, historical, and world-constituting. The machine that would think must first learn to symbolize. And to symbolize is not to represent a ready-made world. It is to create a world in which thinking becomes possible.
