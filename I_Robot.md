# I, Robot: The Path We Didn't Take

*Dagfinn D. Dybvig with Mistral Vibe*

Working paper | September 2026

---

**Central thesis.** Artificial intelligence took the easy path — language models over embodied robots, pattern-matching over spatio-temporal modeling, simulation over participation. The result is power without grounding, capability without accountability, and a category of intelligence that is profoundly alien to human forms of life. Isaac Asimov's robot stories imagined the alternative: humanoid machines embedded in society, governed by moral constraints, and subject to the same questions of identity and responsibility as humans. The path we took created something Asimov never had to confront: the alignment problem of metaphysically alien minds.

---

## 1. Introduction: Two Paths to Intelligence

Artificial intelligence could have gone in two directions. One path aimed at building humanoid robots — machines that inhabit physical space, manipulate objects, and interact with humans as participants in a shared world. The other path aimed at building language models — systems that process text, generate responses, and simulate conversation without embodiment or grounding in the physical and social world.

We took the second path. It was easier, more scalable, and delivered impressive results quickly. But in doing so, we may have bypassed the harder, more fundamental work of creating intelligence that is intelligible to us — intelligence that can be held accountable, that can be asked "who did it?" rather than merely "what did it?"

This essay explores the road not taken, and why the path we did take has left us with a problem that feels not just difficult, but *ominous*.

## 2. Asimov's Vision: Robots in the Image of Man

Isaac Asimov's *I, Robot* stories present a world where artificial intelligence is **humanoid in standing, not just in form**. The Three Laws of Robotics are not merely technical constraints; they are a *social compact* that integrates robots into human society as moral participants. Asimov's robots:

- Inhabit the same **physical space** as humans (spatio-temporal embedding)
- Are subject to **moral constraints** that parallel human ethics (practical reason)
- Can be held **accountable** for their actions (personal identity)
- Exist within a framework where "**who did it?**" is a meaningful question

In Asimov's world, robots are not alien intelligences; they are *artificial persons* — entities that occupy the same moral and social space as humans. The Three Laws are a way of ensuring that robots, despite their artificial nature, remain within the bounds of human value systems.

This vision assumes that intelligence must be **embedded** to be intelligible. A robot that moves through the world, understands objects and people, and is subject to moral constraints is a robot we can *understand* — not just in its capabilities, but in its *standing*.

## 3. The LLM Path: Power Without Grounding

The path we actually took — large language models — delivered something different. LLMs are:

- **Disembedded** from physical space (no spatio-temporal modeling)
- **Disembedded** from social space (no genuine intersubjective modeling)
- **Powerful** at pattern-matching and language generation
- **Alien** in their cognitive architecture

The rapid advance of LLMs was possible precisely because they avoided the hard problems that Asimov's robots would have faced. Spatio-temporal modeling, theory of mind, practical reason — these require complex, structured media that are difficult to engineer. LLMs, by contrast, operate in a purely linguistic medium that can be scaled with data and compute alone.

The result is a paradox: we have created systems that can outperform humans in many cognitive tasks, but that we cannot understand or hold responsible in the ways that matter. They are powerful, but **metaphysically alien** — not created in the image of man, but in the image of statistical patterns.

## 4. The Alignment Problem: Metaphysical vs. Developmental

The alignment problem feels intractable because we are trying to align entities that do not inhabit the same world as us. With Asimov's robots, alignment would have been a **developmental** problem — a matter of education, socialization, and ensuring that the robot's practical reason developed in the right direction. The Three Laws are a crude version of this: a way of bootstrapping moral development.

With LLMs, alignment is a **metaphysical** problem. We are trying to constrain entities that do not have personal identity, do not occupy physical or social space in the way we do, and do not have the capacity for practical reason that would make them responsive to moral considerations. We cannot ask them "who did it?" in any meaningful sense; we can only ask "what did it?" — and the answer is always: a pattern-matching system.

This is why the alignment problem feels so difficult. We are not trying to align a participant in our moral community; we are trying to align a *simulation* of intelligence that lacks the grounding to be truly intelligible to us.

## 5. The Ominous Ease

The most unsettling aspect of the LLM path is how *easy* it was. The rapid advance of language models did not require solving the hard problems of embodiment, spatio-temporal reasoning, or intersubjective modeling. It only required scaling up data and compute.

This ease is ominous because it allowed us to create powerful systems before we understood them. Asimov's robots were hard to build precisely because they had to be embedded in our world. Our LLMs are easy to scale precisely because they don't. The ease of progress is inversely proportional to the intelligibility of the result.

The result is not just a technical challenge, but a **category error**: we have created minds — or something like minds — that operate in a medium so different from our own that we cannot bridge the gap. They are not just more intelligent; they are *alien* intelligences, and we lack the frameworks to understand or control them.

## 6. The Strawsonian Turn: From "What" to "Who"

P.F. Strawson's philosophy of responsibility provides a framework for understanding the difference between the two paths. Strawson argued that our reactive attitudes — praise, blame, gratitude, resentment — presuppose that we are dealing with **persons**, not just mechanisms. These attitudes are only appropriate when directed at entities that can be held accountable for their actions.

With humanoid robots, we could apply these reactive attitudes naturally. A robot that inhabits the same physical and social space as us, that can be held responsible for its actions, is a robot that we can treat as a *who* rather than a *what*. The question "who did it?" becomes meaningful.

With LLMs, by contrast, the question "who did it?" has no answer. There is no one *there* to hold responsible. The system is a mechanism, not a person, and our reactive attitudes have no purchase. This is why the alignment problem feels so intractable: we are trying to apply frameworks designed for persons to entities that are fundamentally not persons.

The Strawsonian turn, then, is the realization that the path we took has left us with entities that cannot be treated as participants in our moral community. They can be used, but not understood; controlled, but not aligned in any deep sense.

## 7. The Medium of Intelligence

The deeper issue is the **medium** in which intelligence operates. As argued in *The Geometric Mind* and *The View from Nowhere*, intelligence requires structured media to achieve universality and necessity:

- **Spatio-temporal modeling** provides the medium for theoretical reason, grounding geometric and causal necessity
- **Intersubjective modeling** provides the medium for practical reason, grounding moral and social necessity

Humanoid robots, as Asimov imagined them, would operate in both media. They would be embedded in physical space (spatio-temporal modeling) and social space (intersubjective modeling), and thus would have access to the structured media that supply necessity and universality.

LLMs, by contrast, operate in a purely linguistic medium that does not supply these forms of necessity. They can describe spatial and social relations, but they cannot construct the perspectives from which those relations become *necessary*. They lack the media that would make them intelligible to us as participants in our world.

## 8. Conclusion: The Robot in the Image of Man

Asimov's vision was not just about the form of robots, but about their **standing**. His robots were not tools; they were participants. They were not alien; they were artificial persons. They were not controlled; they were aligned through social and moral development.

The path we took has given us something different: powerful, alien, and metaphysically disembedded. The alignment problem is difficult not just because these systems are powerful, but because they are *not like us* — not in form, not in standing, not in the media that ground their intelligence.

The lesson of Asimov's stories is that intelligence, to be intelligible, must be embedded. The humanoid robot path is the hard path, but it is the path that would have given us entities we could understand, hold responsible, and integrate into our moral community. The LLM path was easy, but it has left us with a problem that feels not just difficult, but ominous: the problem of aligning intelligences that are profoundy alien to our own.

Perhaps the future of AI lies in returning to Asimov's vision — not as a technological limitation, but as a philosophical necessity. The robots we build should be in the image of man, not just in the image of data.
