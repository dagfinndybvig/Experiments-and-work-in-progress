# The Fork and the Form: Hume's Analytic Necessity and the Constitution of Mathematical Ideas

*Dagfinn D. Dybvig and Mistral Vibe*

## 1. Introduction: The Debate the Trilogy Inherited

The preceding papers in this series operate in a philosophical space shaped by three figures: Fodor, Wittgenstein, and Cassirer. Fodor represents the rationalist inheritance — innate structure, reason as the source of cognitive competence, a Language of Thought substantially given by nature. Wittgenstein represents the empiricist correction — structure acquired through public practice, meaning arising in use, no private Mentalese behind the games. Cassirer represents the neo-Kantian synthesis that superseded both — forms that constitute experience, neither read off from it (against Hume) nor innate in the subject (against Descartes).

This essay makes explicit what the trilogy left implicit: the argument bears directly on the empiricism-rationalism debate, and specifically on the Hume-Descartes opposition, but it does not take sides in that opposition. It works in the post-Kantian space that replaced it, and it pushes against Hume on the point where Hume thought he had secured necessity from the empiricist critique — the analytic side of his fork.

The argument has three bearings on the classical debate. First, the first essay's rejection of Fodor is an empiricist move about concept acquisition, but a pragmatic rather than a Humean empiricism. Second, the Cartesian Moment essay separates the rationalist philosopher from the inventor of the symbolic form, and argues that the latter is what mattered — a rejection of rationalism by way of Descartes himself. Third, and most consequentially, the constitutive claim challenges Hume's fork at its strongest point: the analytic necessity of relations of ideas. The asymptote of language-only reasoning, which the preceding essay identified as the empirical signature of the constitutive gap, is the observable trace of Hume's underestimation of what formal forms contribute even to the analytic side.

## 2. Fodor as Rationalist, Wittgenstein as Pragmatic Empiricist

Fodor's Language of Thought hypothesis is rationalist in the classical sense. Cognition requires systematic, recombinable, inferentially consequential structure; Fodor posits this structure as substantially innate — a Mentalese given by nature, not acquired through experience. His concept nativism extends the rationalism to content: most lexical concepts are innate, on the grounds that learning through hypothesis testing presupposes the concepts needed to formulate the hypothesis. This is the rationalist commitment to innate ideas, updated into computational terms.

The first essay's Wittgensteinian critique is an empiricist move, but not a Humean one. Hume's empiricism is about the origin of ideas: all ideas derive from impressions, and complex ideas are combinations of simple ones furnished by sense experience. Wittgenstein's later philosophy is not a theory of the origin of ideas but a theory of meaning: meaning arises in practice, in the weave of words into activity, rule, and purpose. The structure cognition requires is not innate but acquired through participation in public practices — language-games — whose norms are internal to the practice.

This is a pragmatic empiricism, closer to the tradition running through Peirce and the later Wittgenstein than to classical British empiricism. It shares with Hume the rejection of innate ideas, but it differs on what replaces them. For Hume, what replaces innate ideas is impressions and their combinations. For Wittgenstein, what replaces innate Mentalese is public practices and their internalization. The first essay's claim that arithmetic, logic, and proof are acquired languages of thought — not innate endowments but internalized practices — is Wittgensteinian in exactly this sense: the structure is real, but it is acquired, and what is acquired is a practice, not a set of impressions.

So the first essay does not side with Hume against Fodor. It sides with Wittgenstein against both. Hume and Fodor share an assumption the Wittgensteinian critique rejects: that the relevant cognitive structure is either given by nature (Fodor) or given by sensation (Hume). Wittgenstein's alternative is that it is given by practice — by public, norm-governed activities that are neither innate nor sensory but social and historical.

## 3. Descartes Against the Rationalist

The Cartesian Moment essay does something quietly radical to the rationalist inheritance. The standard Descartes is the rationalist: innate ideas, the cogito, clear and distinct perception, reason as the source of certain knowledge. The essay does not deny this reading of the *Discourse*. It argues that the *Discourse* is not where Descartes' real contribution lies.

The *Geometry* is the consequential text, on the essay's reading, and it is not rationalist in the classical sense. The coordinate method does not secure knowledge through reason operating on innate ideas. It makes a domain — spatial extension — formally computable through a notation. The *Discourse* seeks foundations in the self-transparent thinking subject; the *Geometry* constructs a medium in which the subject is unnecessary to the manipulation. The method requires a self; the notation does not. "Cogitation without cogito" is the essay's formulation: the formal system thinks, and the thinker is optional.

This separates the rationalist philosopher from the inventor of the symbolic form. Descartes the rationalist wrote the *Discourse*; Descartes the constitutive inventor wrote the *Geometry*. The essay argues that the latter transformed European civilization and the former did not. The rationalist project — securing knowledge through reason operating on clear and distinct ideas — is treated as a genuine but secondary achievement, addressed to the problem of epistemic certainty. The constitutive project — making extension computable through notation — is treated as the foundational achievement, addressed to the problem of making a domain formally operable.

This is a rejection of rationalism by way of Descartes himself. It does not say rationalism is wrong; it says rationalism is not what did the work. The work was done by the invention of a symbolic form, which is neither rationalist nor empiricist in the classical sense. It is the Cassirerian move: a form that constitutes its objects, not a reason that discovers them (rationalism) or a sensation that supplies them (empiricism).

## 4. Hume's Fork and the Analytic Side

Hume's fork divides propositions into relations of ideas and matters of fact. Relations of ideas — mathematics, logic, algebra — are necessary, a priori, and deniable only by contradiction. Matters of fact — empirical claims about the world — are contingent, a posteriori, and known through experience. The problem of induction is specifically about matters of fact: no amount of observed regularity can yield necessary connection, because the gap between "has always happened" and "must happen" is a gap in kind, not degree.

The crucial point, often understated, is that Hume grants necessity to the analytic side. Mathematics is not vulnerable to the problem of induction. The truths of arithmetic and geometry are necessary because they are relations among ideas, not claims about the world. Hume is an empiricist about the *origin* of ideas — all ideas derive from impressions — but a rationalist about the *necessity* of relations among ideas once formed. The fork is not a blanket skepticism about necessity; it is a restriction of necessity to the analytic domain, with the synthetic domain left to the mercies of induction.

This means the essays' asymptote claim cannot be parallel to Hume's problem of induction in any simple way. The asymptote concerns mathematics — the derivative, the proof, the formal derivation — which on Hume's framework is a relation of ideas, analytic, necessary. If the model has the ideas and can track their relations, it should get necessity. Why would there be an asymptote at all?

The preceding essay's answer was that the model does not have the ideas — it has the textual traces of the discourse about them. But this answer, framed in Wittgensteinian terms as the gap between pattern and practice, needs to be connected to Hume's framework explicitly. The connection reveals that the constitutive claim is not an instance of Hume's problem but a challenge to his fork at its strongest point.

## 5. The Constitution of Ideas, Not Just Their Relations

Hume's fork assumes the ideas are given. The ideas derive from impressions, on his account, and necessity lives in the relations among them. The derivative, on Hume's framework, would be a relation of ideas: once the ideas of velocity, instant, and limit are in place, the necessary relations among them are analytic. Hume would grant the necessity of the calculus; he would classify it as a relation of ideas.

The essays' constitutive claim denies this assumption. The derivative was not available as a relation of ideas before the notation existed. The notation does not merely make explicit the necessary relations among pre-existing ideas; it constitutes the ideas themselves. Before calculus, there was no idea of instantaneous velocity as a coherent mathematical object — not because the idea was hidden, but because the formal medium that would constitute it did not yet exist. The pre-calculus thinker could speak of average velocity, but the instantaneous velocity had no coherent referent. The notation *dy/dx* does not express a relation among given ideas; it constitutes the idea whose relations are then necessary.

This is the Kantian move against Hume, and the essays are neo-Kantian in exactly this way. Kant's synthetic a priori was the response to the fact that mathematical truths seem necessary (like analytic truths) yet extend knowledge (like synthetic truths). The essays' constitutive claim has the same structure: the derivative is necessary — it is mathematical — but it is not analytic in Hume's sense of being a relation among pre-existing ideas. It is constituted by a form that creates the ideas. Hume's fork assumes the ideas are given and asks about the status of their relations; the essays say the form gives you the ideas, and without the form, you do not have the ideas whose relations are necessary.

The implication for the asymptote is precise. A language model trained on the textual traces of mathematical practice can learn the patterns of the discourse — the ways mathematicians talk about derivatives, the forms proofs take, the standard moves in a calculation. On Hume's framework, if the model has the ideas and can track their relations, it should get necessity. The essays' answer is that the model does not have the ideas, because the ideas are constituted by the formal form, and the formal form is not the linguistic medium. The model has the discourse about the ideas, not the ideas. The asymptote is the empirical signature of this: the model approximates the behavior of operating on the ideas without possessing the ideas, and the approximation is asymptotic because the gap is in kind — between discourse and possession — not in degree.

## 6. The Asymptote as the Trace of Hume's Underestimation

Hume thought he had secured necessity on the analytic side. Relations of ideas are necessary, and the problem of induction does not touch them. The essays say that even the analytic side requires the constitutive move: you need the form to have the ideas whose relations are necessary. Hume took the ideas as given — derived from impressions, combined into complex ideas — and asked about the status of their relations. The essays say the ideas are not given; they are constituted by symbolic forms, and a system without the form does not have the ideas, only their descriptions.

The asymptote of language-only reasoning is the observable trace of this. If Hume were right that the analytic side is secure once the ideas are in place, then a language model that has learned the ideas (from the textual traces of mathematical practice) should be able to track their necessary relations and get validity. The fact that it approximates without achieving — that it produces proof-shaped text that is usually right but not necessarily right, that it hallucinates steps and fails on long derivations — is evidence that the ideas are not in place, that learning the discourse about the ideas is not the same as possessing them.

This is why the asymptote is not parallel to Hume's problem of induction but is a challenge to Hume's fork at its strongest point. Hume's problem is that necessity cannot be grounded in the synthetic domain. The essays' point is that necessity in the analytic domain depends on the constitution of the ideas, and the constitution of the ideas depends on symbolic forms that are not available in the linguistic medium. Hume's fork assumes the ideas are given; the essays say they are constituted, and the constituting medium is not language.

The "both" prescription — internal reasoning joined to external formal systems — is, in this light, not a compromise between empiricism and rationalism. It is the Kantian-Cassirerian position. Both sensibility and understanding, both language and formal form. Language provides the medium in which the formal forms are described, coordinated, and interpreted; the formal forms provide the medium in which the ideas are constituted. Neither alone is sufficient, because the ideas are not in the linguistic medium (against pure empiricism about the analytic) and not innate in the subject (against rationalism). They are constituted by forms that must be acquired.

## 7. Descartes, Hume, and the Exit

There is a final bearing on the classical debate that the essays make visible. Hume's problem of induction is, within his framework, in principle unsolvable. Necessity cannot be grounded in the synthetic domain; the uniformity of nature cannot be proved. The essays' asymptote is not unsolvable in this way. It is sidestep-able by entering the formal medium. Hume has no exit from the problem of induction; the essays have an exit from the asymptote, and it is the one Descartes took when he stopped writing the *Discourse* and wrote the *Geometry*.

The difference is the constitutive move, which Hume lacked. Hume's framework gives you impressions and their combinations, and asks whether necessity can be grounded among them. The constitutive move gives you a way to possess necessity without deriving it from regularity: you institute a rule, and the rule constitutes the ideas whose relations are then necessary. You do not get necessity by scaling induction; you get it by entering a form.

This is the neo-Kantian resolution that the essays inherit. Kant's response to Hume was that the categories constitute experience, so necessity is not read off from regularity but supplied by the form of experience. The essays' response is the Cassirerian descendant: symbolic forms constitute their objects, so mathematical necessity is not read off from the patterns of mathematical discourse but supplied by the formal form. The asymptote is what you get when a system has the discourse without the form — the patterns without the constitution, the textual traces without the ideas.

## 8. Conclusion: The Post-Kantian Space

The trilogy does not take sides in the empiricism-rationalism debate. It works in the post-Kantian space that superseded it, and the constitutive claim is the neo-Kantian move that makes the classical opposition less interesting than the question of what forms constitute what ideas.

The first essay's rejection of Fodor is not a Humean empiricism but a Wittgensteinian pragmatic empiricism: structure is acquired through practice, not through impressions or innate endowment. The Cartesian Moment's rereading of Descartes separates the rationalist philosopher from the constitutive inventor and argues that the latter mattered. The constitutive claim challenges Hume's fork at its strongest point: the analytic necessity of relations of ideas depends on the constitution of the ideas, and the constitution depends on symbolic forms that are not available in the linguistic medium.

The asymptote of language-only reasoning is the empirical signature of this dependence. It is not a failure of scale but the trace of a difference in kind — between discourse and possession, between pattern and rule, between the textual traces of a practice and the practice itself. Hume thought the analytic side was secure because the ideas were given and their relations were necessary. The essays say the ideas are not given; they are constituted, and a system without the constituting form has the discourse but not the ideas. The machine that would cross the asymptote must do what Descartes did: not reason longer in the medium it has, but enter the medium that constitutes the ideas whose relations it needs.
