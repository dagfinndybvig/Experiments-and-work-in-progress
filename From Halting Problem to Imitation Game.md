# More than Imitation: Turing, Learning Machines and RSI

*Dagfinn D. Dybvig and Claude Fable 5*

## 1. Introduction

Alan Turing is remembered, above all, for a test. In "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), he replaced the question "Can machines think?" with an operational substitute: can a machine imitate human conversation well enough that an interrogator cannot reliably tell it from a person? The imitation game has since become the emblem of Turing's philosophy of mind, the founding thought experiment of artificial intelligence, and — in the era of large language models — a benchmark that many would say has been effectively passed, or at least rendered uninteresting by fluent machine conversation.

This paper argues that the canonical reception gets Turing's priorities almost exactly backwards. The imitation game was never the centre of Turing's mature view of machine intelligence; it was the evaluation protocol for a research programme whose real substance lay elsewhere — in learning machines. That programme is set out in detail in his 1948 National Physical Laboratory report "Intelligent Machinery" (Turing 1948/2004), a document that anticipates connectionist networks, training regimes, and the education of "child machines," and which itself concludes with a restricted imitation game, played over chess, proposed precisely as a way of *evaluating* a machine's acquired competence. The 1950 paper, read with the 1948 report in hand, reveals the same architecture: its longest and final substantive section, "Learning Machines," recapitulates and extends the 1948 programme, and its famous test is offered as the criterion by which the products of that programme should eventually be judged.

The two papers, in other words, refer to each other and complete each other. The 1948 report supplies the constructive programme and sketches the evaluative game; the 1950 paper elaborates the game and restates the programme. Taken together they constitute a unified mature view in which imitation is subordinate to learning: the question that mattered to Turing was not whether machines could *appear* intelligent, but how machines could *become* intelligent — and, in a striking passage on "supercritical" minds, whether machine intelligence might become self-amplifying. That last suggestion, I shall argue, makes Turing a more direct ancestor than is usually recognized of I. J. Good's "intelligence explosion" (Good 1965) and of what is now discussed under the heading of recursive self-improvement (RSI).

The argument proceeds in four steps. Section 2 reconstructs the 1948 report as a learning-machines programme with an imitation test attached. Section 3 rereads the 1950 paper as the same structure inverted for a philosophical audience. Section 4 states the subordination thesis and situates it against existing scholarship, arguing that while its components are individually well documented, the structural claim itself has not been made. Section 5 traces the supercritical passage forward to Good and to contemporary RSI debates, and considers what follows for how we should read Turing today.

## 2. Intelligent Machinery (1948): learning first, imitation last

"Intelligent Machinery," written in the summer of 1948 for the National Physical Laboratory and unpublished in Turing's lifetime, opens with a declaration of intent: "I propose to investigate the question as to whether it is possible for machinery to show intelligent behaviour" (Turing 1948/2004, 410). What follows is not a philosophical defence of the possibility of machine thought but an engineering prospectus. Turing surveys the objections he expects ("contrary views"), dismisses them briskly, and then devotes the body of the report to the question of *how* intelligent machinery might actually be produced.

His answer has three components that will be immediately familiar to a modern reader. First, *unorganised machines*: networks of simple neuron-like units, randomly connected, which Turing offers as a model of the infant human cortex. Copeland and Proudfoot (1996) have shown that these "A-type" and "B-type" machines constitute a genuine anticipation of connectionism, complete with the idea that a network's behaviour is determined by modifiable connections rather than by an explicit programme. Second, *training*: unorganised machines are to become organised "by suitable interfering training" — Turing discusses reward and punishment signals, the modification of the machine's internal organisation through experience, and the analogy with the education of a child. Third, the *child machine* itself: rather than attempting to hand-build an adult mind, one should construct a comparatively simple machine endowed with the capacity to learn, and then educate it. Intelligence, on this picture, is not installed; it is grown.

The report's structure is as significant as its content. Having laid out the constructive programme, Turing turns at the end to the question of how one would *know* that the programme had succeeded. His answer is a small experiment. Noting that "the learning of languages would be the most impressive" field in which to demonstrate machine intelligence, "since it is the most human of these activities," but judging it to "depend rather too much on sense organs and locomotion to be feasible" with foreseeable hardware, he settles on chess (Turing 1948/2004; cf. Gonçalves 2023a). He then describes a three-person experiment: a chess-playing machine (simulated, if need be, by a human following rules on paper), a weak human player, and an interrogator-judge who must determine which opponent is which. If the judge "may find it quite difficult to tell" the machine from the human player (Turing 1948/2004, 431), the machine has exhibited intelligence in the restricted domain.

This is, unmistakably, an imitation game — restricted to chess, but identical in logic to the 1950 test: indistinguishability from a human, under examination, as the criterion of intelligence. Proudfoot (2013) and Copeland and Proudfoot (2009) have documented this 1948 version, alongside a third variant from a 1952 BBC radio discussion, and have argued that all three are variants of a single experimental design. Gonçalves (2023a; 2024) has added the historical mechanism behind the change of domain: chess had been Turing's preferred arena for machine intelligence since at least 1946, and the 1950 shift to open conversation is best understood as a response to critics — Geoffrey Jefferson and Michael Polanyi prominent among them — who objected that chess, being exhaustively rule-specifiable, could not evidence genuine thought.

For present purposes, the crucial point is positional. In the 1948 report the imitation experiment is not the foundation of the argument; it is the *terminus*. It appears after the learning programme has been laid out, as the answer to the question "how would we test the result?" The report's centre of gravity is the child machine. The game is its examination.

## 3. Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950): the same structure, inverted

The 1950 paper presents itself differently. It opens with the test, spends its middle sections refuting nine objections, and arrives only in its final section at the topic of learning. Read in isolation — as it almost always is — the paper therefore looks like a defence of a behavioural criterion of intelligence, with a speculative coda about how such a machine might be built. This is the reading enshrined in the philosophical literature, where debate has centred on whether passing the test is constitutive of, or merely evidence for, intelligence (Moor 2001; Sterrett 2000).

But the proportions of the paper tell a different story. Section 7, "Learning Machines," is the longest constructive discussion in the paper, and it is a compressed restatement of the 1948 programme. The correspondence is point-for-point. The proposal: "Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child's?" (Turing 1950, 456). The method: subject the child machine to "an appropriate course of education." The explicitly evolutionary gloss: the structure of the child machine corresponds to hereditary material, its modifications to mutation, and the experimenter's judgement to natural selection (Turing 1950, 456). Even Turing's hedged catholicism about methods — that both explicit programming and training-based approaches should be pursued — carries over from the report (Leiber 2006). Turing nowhere in 1950 cites the unpublished 1948 report, which he could hardly do; but the "Learning Machines" section is functionally a published abstract of it, as Copeland's editorial commentary in *The Essential Turing* makes clear (Copeland 2004).

Moreover, the learning-machines section is not a coda; it is the paper's answer to its own residual question. Turing concedes, with disarming candour, that he has "no very convincing arguments of a positive nature" that machines will pass his test — only refutations of arguments that they cannot. The positive case he does offer is the child-machine programme: the test will eventually be passed *because* machines can be made to learn. Imitation marks the finish line; learning is the engine expected to reach it. His concluding prediction is framed in exactly these terms — it is by teaching machines, beginning with domains like chess or the understanding of English, that "machines will eventually compete with men in all purely intellectual fields" (Turing 1950, 460).

There is a further, less noticed structural link. Immediately *before* introducing the child machine, Turing reaches for a physical analogy: the atomic pile. Minds, he suggests, are like fissile material. Present an idea to a "subcritical" mind and it gives rise, on average, to less than one idea in reply; the disturbance dies away. In a "supercritical" mind, an idea "may give rise to a whole 'theory' consisting of secondary, tertiary and more remote ideas." Human minds, he remarks, are mostly subcritical; and he then poses the question in mechanical form: "Can a machine be made to be supercritical?" (Turing 1950, 454). The placement is deliberate. The supercritical question — when does intelligence become generative, self-sustaining, amplifying — is what motivates the turn to learning machines in the first place. A machine that merely retrieves canned responses is subcritical by construction. Only a machine that learns could, even in principle, cross the threshold.

The 1950 paper, then, has the same deep structure as the 1948 report, with the order of presentation inverted for a philosophical audience: test first, programme last in 1950; programme first, test last in 1948. The inversion was rhetorically astute — Gonçalves (2023b; 2024) argues persuasively that the 1950 paper is shaped throughout by the need to meet sceptical interlocutors on their own ground — but it has exacted a century-scale interpretive cost. Philosophy took the bait and spent seventy years on the test.

## 4. The subordination thesis

The claim I wish to press is stronger than the (now well-established) observation that the 1948 report contains a precursor of the Turing test, and stronger too than the observation that the 1950 paper recapitulates the 1948 learning programme. It is a claim about the architecture of Turing's mature view: *the imitation game is subordinate to the learning-machines programme*. The test is not Turing's theory of intelligence; it is his measurement instrument. The theory — insofar as the engineer in Turing permitted himself one — is that intelligence is the product of training applied to initially unorganised but suitably structured machines, and that its hallmark is generativity: the supercritical cascade of ideas, not the successful impersonation.

Each component of this reading has support in the scholarship, but the structural claim itself does not appear to have been made. Copeland and Proudfoot (1996; 2009) established the connectionist content of the 1948 report and catalogued the test's three versions; Proudfoot (2013) argued that the 1948 and 1952 versions are "essential to understanding the test," and has elsewhere developed a response-dependence interpretation of intelligence on Turing's behalf. Sterrett (2000) showed that even within the 1950 paper the game is doubled, and that the differences matter. Leiber (2006) noted — observing that the point had "largely gone unnoticed" — that the 1948 proposals about unorganised machines and the child machine are proposals about how to make *Turing-test passers*. Gonçalves, in the most sustained recent treatment (2023a; 2023b; 2024), reads the test as a Galilean thought experiment that was methodically varied under criticism, and observes that it "suggests a hypothesis on machine learning."

All of this is congenial to the subordination thesis; none of it states it. The literature's accumulated result is that the two papers are *connected*; the present claim is that they are *ordered*. Reading 1948 and 1950 as a single two-part work — programme and protocol — has at least three consequences worth making explicit.

First, it dissolves the long-standing dispute over whether Turing intended the test as a *definition* of intelligence or as mere *evidence*. On the unified reading the question is malformed: the test is an evaluation procedure for a developmental process, in precisely the way a final examination is neither a definition of education nor mere inductive evidence of it, but the institutionalized criterion by which a course of training is certified. Turing the engineer needed an acceptance test; Turing the rhetorician needed one his critics could not dismiss as question-begging. The imitation game served both needs. It was never asked to bear the metaphysical weight later placed upon it.

Second, the unified reading explains otherwise puzzling features of the 1950 text: why the "Learning Machines" section is so long and so confident while the defence of the test is so breezy; why Turing's prediction concerns the year 2000 and an education process rather than a programming milestone; why the paper ends not with conversation but with chess and the teaching of English as the two recommended starting points — the very pair of fields weighed against each other in the 1948 report.

Third, and most consequentially, it relocates Turing in the genealogy of present-day AI. If the mature Turing is the theorist of learning machines evaluated by imitation, then the modern deep-learning pipeline — train a comparatively unstructured network on experience, then evaluate it against human performance on benchmarks — is not a distant descendant of the Turing test. It is the 1948–1950 programme, executed. Turing anticipated not only the criterion by which we judge large language models but the developmental logic by which they are produced, including the division of labour between architecture ("the structure of the child machine") and training ("the education process"). This makes him a pioneer of a more radical kind than the standard reception allows.

## 5. Supercritical machines: from the atomic pile to RSI

The unified reading also changes the status of the supercritical passage. Detached from the learning programme, it reads as a rhetorical flourish. Attached to it, it reads as the programme's furthest extrapolation — and as the seed of the intelligence-explosion tradition.

The lineage runs through I. J. Good, Turing's friend and colleague in the Bletchley Park years and afterwards at Manchester (Hodges 1983). In "Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine," Good defined an ultraintelligent machine as one that surpasses all human intellectual activities, and drew the now-famous consequence: "Since the design of machines is one of these intellectual activities, an ultraintelligent machine could design even better machines; there would then unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion'" (Good 1965, 33). The structural identity with Turing's analogy is exact, down to the choice of metaphor: an explosion is what a critical mass produces. Turing's supercritical mind is one whose outputs (ideas) exceed its inputs; Good's ultraintelligent machine is one whose outputs (machine designs) exceed its own design. Good closes the loop that Turing left open — the ideas the machine generates are ideas about machines — but the threshold concept, the chain-reaction dynamics, and the nuclear imagery are all in the 1950 paper, three pages before the child machine is introduced. The biographical connection between the two men is well documented; the textual debt, surprisingly, has received little close analysis, and the present reading suggests it deserves more.

From Good the line runs forward, through Chalmers's (2010) philosophical reconstruction of the singularity argument, to the contemporary discussion of recursive self-improvement. RSI, in current usage, rarely means a machine rewriting its own code overnight; it means a feedback loop in which AI systems contribute increasingly to the research, engineering, and evaluation that produce successor systems — coding, debugging, running experiments, assessing results — until the development cycle is substantially self-accelerating. The question practitioners now ask is, almost verbatim, Turing's: not "can the machine imitate us?" but "when does the process go critical?"

Seen from 2026, then, the two questions Turing posed have fared very differently. The imitation question has been, if not answered, then deflated: fluent conversational indistinguishability is approximately achieved, and its achievement has settled few of the philosophical disputes attached to it. The supercritical question has sharpened into the central open problem of the field's trajectory and of its governance. Yet the two questions remain related in exactly the way the unified reading predicts: as capability and criterion. The deep irony of the present moment is that imitation has been demoted to *infrastructure* — benchmark suites, human-preference evaluations, red-team interrogations are all institutionalized imitation games — deployed to measure progress along the curve whose endpoint is the supercritical regime. Evaluation in service of learning: the subordination Turing built into his programme in 1948 is now the operating structure of the industry.

## 6. Conclusion

Two qualifications should be entered. First, the unified reading is a rational reconstruction from two texts written for different audiences two years apart, not a claim about a doctrine Turing ever stated as such; the 1948 report was an internal document, and Turing left no programmatic statement linking the two papers. Second, nothing here implies that the supercritical transition is imminent or inevitable — Turing himself posed it as a question, and questions it remains, gated in practice by compute, data, capital, and human oversight.

With those caveats, the conclusion stands. "Intelligent Machinery" and "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" are two halves of one argument: a constructive programme for growing intelligence through learning, paired with an adversarial protocol for certifying it. The imitation game, celebrated as Turing's central contribution, was the subordinate half. The learning-machines programme, long treated as a speculative appendix, was the substance — and its furthest extrapolation, the supercritical machine, named the question that now dominates the field. Turing was not merely the author of a clever test that machines have finally passed. He was the first theorist of the training-and-evaluation loop, and the first to ask when that loop becomes a chain reaction. We have spent seventy-five years grading machines on his examination. The syllabus was his too.
